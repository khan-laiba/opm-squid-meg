A. Estimands and conventions

The primary metric. Known-topography detectability is d = √(sᵀC⁻¹s): the output signal-to-noise ratio of the optimal matched filter for a source of known field pattern s in noise of covariance C. It is computed with the true (oracle) covariance and rank-aware whitening for every target and every array. Arrays are compared target by target. The adult comparison reports the median over targets of the paired ratio dOPM/dNeuromag, not the ratio of the two systems' median detectabilities. D = 20 log10(dOPM/dNeuromag) is the same quantity in dB, positive when the OPM is ahead. D does not depend on the dipole moment. The comparator is Neuromag's 306 channels unless stated.

Two medians, one metric. The adult analyses report the unweighted median over all 7,661 targets, the 558 on the medial wall included. The smaller heads use an area-weighted median over cortical targets, the medial wall excluded, because their cortices differ in size and their targets in density. At the adult's measured head position the dense array's ratio is 1.144 unweighted over all targets, 1.148 without the medial wall and, area-weighted without it, 1.158; its D is +1.17 dB under the adult convention and +1.27 dB under the pediatric one. A 1 % change of the ratio is 0.086 dB, and 1 dB is a ratio of 1.122. Table S1 gives the adult's primary comparison in both forms.

The reference placement of the adult. Every Δ is taken against the adult placed by the same rule, because the adult's own D depends on the placement: +1.27 dB at its measured head position, +1.00 dB at top contact, +1.01 dB laterally centred and raised to contact, +0.87 dB at true contact, +1.27 and +1.28 dB in its own helmets scaled with the head (centred and laterally centred; their scale factor is 1.000), and +1.28 dB in the helmet fitted at its own gap. Where a difference of two pooled medians is used instead of Δ, the text says so. Δ is reported descriptively, without a test or a margin.

Peak-channel and mean-power SNR. Peak-channel SNR lets magnetometers and gradiometers compete on equal terms, because each channel is divided by its own noise. It is the closest analogue of the clinical studies' sensor-level SNRs, with one difference: a clinical baseline standard deviation holds physiological noise as well as sensor and room noise, whereas the model's holds only what the noise model contains. Mean-power SNR is the channel-averaged SNR of Goldenholz et al. (2009), also used by Matsubara et al. (2026).

Plug-in detectability. The plug-in filter is built from a Ledoit–Wolf covariance estimate (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004) and evaluated under the true covariance, so it cannot exceed the oracle value. The estimate uses 780 or 4,680 independent samples, the time-bandwidth equivalents of 10 and 60 s of data in this band, drawn for all arrays from one common noise realization. The estimate from 4,680 samples is the declared 60-s scenario (independent samples, which do not establish the effective sample size of a measured coloured-noise baseline); the one from 780 samples shows how a short baseline penalizes the array with more channels.

Intervals. Neighbouring cortical targets are correlated, so no interval resamples targets as if they were independent, except the adult's frequency-band comparison, which resamples targets and therefore has narrower intervals than the primary comparison (Table S15). All intervals are percentile intervals. In the spike study the location is the statistical unit: S50 ratios carry location-bootstrap intervals, and detection differences are tested with the sign-flip test on per-location differences in detection counts, reported exactly over all sign patterns (section G.5). The resample count of every interval family is in Table S15.

B. The spherical benchmark

Reimplementation. The analytical model of Jas et al. (2026) was reimplemented with their definitions: a tangential current dipole of 30 nAm in a spherical conductor (head radius 95 mm, brain radius 80 mm), point magnetometers measuring the radial field on the scalp (OPM) and 18 mm above it (SQUID), single-channel SNR equal to the peak field over the noise σ, and σOPM = η σSQUID. The equal-SNR depth is the exact root of the preprint's Eq. 3. The peak field was checked against the full Sarvas field maximized over the sensor sphere (largest relative difference 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁵) and against MNE-Python's sphere model (5.4 × 10⁻⁸).

The reimplementation reproduces the preprint. At η = 3 the exact equal-SNR depth is 27.665 mm. The preprint prints 28 mm in its text and caption, and its figure draws 27.53 mm; the difference is the preprint's rounding and grid, not a difference of model. A crossing exists only for η between 1.683 and 5.309 (printed 1.7 and 5.3). Only η enters the equal-SNR depth: scaling both noises together leaves it at 27.665 mm.

Transfer to the real head. On the real head, the peak field of the best OPM over that of the best Neuromag magnetometer falls with depth as the sphere predicts once the sphere is given the real median standoffs, 7.0 mm for the OPMs and 29.8 mm for the magnetometers. For the dense array the ratio falls from 5.41 in the 10–15 mm bin to 2.28 at 55–60 mm, against 5.26 and 2.09 for the sphere.

The bridge between the two models uses the sphere's own metric: peak-channel SNR against the magnetometers, with sensor noise only. With it, the realistic equal-SNR depth at η = 3 is 29.6 mm for the site-matched array and 31.8 mm for the dense array, against 29.0 mm for the sphere with the real standoffs. For η from 2.5 to 4.5 the real arrays' depths lie within −0.8 to +5.4 mm of the sphere's. Outside that window they differ. The OPMs are ahead at every depth for η ≤ 2.25, where the sphere still predicts a deep crossing (48.8 mm at η = 2.25), because the real peak-field ratio levels off at depth (2.35 at 60–65 mm) while the sphere's keeps falling (2.02). Neuromag's magnetometers are ahead at every depth for η ≥ 5.0 (site-matched) or 5.5 (dense). The sphere is therefore right about the geometry of magnetometers near its reference η (Figure S1). Against Neuromag's 204 planar gradiometers the sensor-noise-only ratio barely changes with depth, so the sphere describes only the magnetometer part of the comparison. What it leaves open is η itself, which the explicit noise model of section C replaces.

Head size in the sphere. For head size, the preprint's newborn, 1-year, 8-year and adult spheres were placed in a SQUID shell that follows each head at 18 mm, as in the preprint, and, as a new contrast, all in the adult shell of radius 113 mm. With the size-following shell the equal-SNR depth at η = 3 hardly moves from adult to newborn (27.7 to 30.8 mm), but the normalized depth rises from 15.8% to 49.6%, because the brain and the extracerebral layer (7 to 15 mm in the preprint's heads) shrink. With one adult shell for every head, size and fit are confounded: the OPM is then ahead at every depth of the newborn and 1-year brains up to η = 8.7 and 4.2. The sphere's prediction for children is thus conditional on a shell that follows the head at a constant gap. The panels of this head-size benchmark are shown on S4, the repository's own report of the smaller heads.

C. The noise model: details, validation and sensitivity analyses

C.1 Components and calibration

Every array sees identical noise sources in one analysis band of 1 to 40 Hz (zero-phase Butterworth filter of order 4; equivalent noise bandwidth 35.1 Hz). The three components are sensor noise, the cortical background and the room field.

Sensor noise is white: 3.5 fT/√Hz for the Neuromag magnetometers and 3.6 fT/(cm √Hz) for the gradiometers, the typical values of the TRIUX datasheet (Elekta Neuromag, n.d.), and 15 fT/√Hz for the OPMs in the primary analysis, swept from 7 to 30 fT/√Hz. The OPM value is a declared parameter, not a measured device value. It is the middle of the sweep and within the range of sensor noise quoted by the clinical OPM studies, although the clinical studies state no band for these figures and the device specifications behind them begin at 1 Hz or above (Osborne et al., 2018; S7); the top of the sweep is close to the empty-room floor read from the preprint's recording (a reading of its Fig. 9 that assumes its unstated dB reference is 1 fT²/Hz). The comparisons are reported across the sweep.

The cortical background consists of 1,755 independent dipoles normal to the cortex on a 7-mm grid, weighted by cortical area. Like the targets, it omits the cortex within 4 mm of the inner skull (8.7% of the vertices), the sources nearest to every sensor; section C.6 adds that cortex back. One scale was fitted so that the median good gradiometer's in-band variance equals the measured brain noise of the sample recording: the in-band variance of the pre-stimulus windows of its 320 stimuli, which begin soon after the previous stimulus and so hold task-related rather than only resting activity, minus that of the empty-room recording, the channel marked bad excluded. Spatially correlated variants used correlation lengths of 5 and 10 mm.

The room field is an external field of 8 terms (uniform plus linear gradient) fitted to the empty-room recording and passed through each array's coil integration.

The covariance in closed form. Each array's oracle covariance is computed without sampling: C = Σs + s² Lbg A LbgT + E Σe ET. Σs is diagonal, each channel's squared white-noise spectral density times the filter's equivalent noise bandwidth (35.1 Hz). Lbg holds the array's lead fields of the 1,755 independent, cortex-normal background dipoles, a greedy Poisson-disk subset of the usable white-surface vertices with 7-mm minimum spacing (seed 2026). A = diag(ai), ai being the area of source i's Voronoi cell on the cortex; the cells partition the 1,878 cm² of usable cortex (107 mm² on average). Source i's moment variance is s² ai, with s² = 0.0354 nAm² per mm² (standard deviation 0.188 nAm × √(ai/mm²); 1.95 nAm root-mean-square over the sources), fitted once so that the good gradiometers' median modelled variance equals their median measured brain noise (37.1 fT/cm RMS), and kept per unit area on every head. E holds the array's responses, through its own coil integration points, to 8 external-field terms (3 uniform, 5 linear-gradient, about (0, 0, 40) mm in the head frame), and Σe is their coefficient covariance, fitted to the band-filtered empty-room recording. Plug-in covariances are Ledoit–Wolf estimates from 780 or 4,680 independent samples, twice the time-bandwidth product of 10 and 60 s.

Projection. P = I − E(EᵀWE)⁻¹EᵀW, with W = Σs⁻¹, is applied to topographies and covariance alike (Ps, PCPᵀ), by the same rule for every array. For Neuromag it acts on all 306 channels jointly, so its magnetometer and gradiometer comparisons after projection use subsets of jointly projected data. Retained ranks: 298 of 306 (99 of 102 magnetometers, 204 of 204 gradiometers), 200 of 208 (dense) and 90 of 98 (site-matched). Whitening drops eigenvalues of the correlation-normalized covariance below 10⁻¹⁰ of the largest. The projection costs Neuromag 0.48% of its detectability, the dense array 2.5% and the site-matched array 6.0%, relative to the room-field condition without projection.

OPM sensitive axes. Each OPM's axis is the normal of the BEM head surface (placed on the MRI scalp, normals from its own triangles) averaged within 15 mm of the site. It deviates from a plane fitted to the dense MRI scalp by a median 1.2° (95th percentile 4.9° site-matched, 7.4° dense). An earlier construction took the adult's stored outer-skin normals, which lie within 5° of the radial direction. With those axes at the same sites the projected dense ratio is 1.073 [1.017, 1.115] instead of 1.115 (0.82 instead of 0.98 at 45–70 mm): nearly radial axes make room-field patterns resemble those of deep sources, so the projection removes more of their signal. With sensor plus brain noise and no projection the two constructions give 1.130 against 1.144.

Neuromag system. The model combines the sample recording's VectorView geometry (102 magnetometers, 204 planar gradiometers), MNE-Python's T3 coil definitions (types 3014 and 3024; accurate integration over 8 points per gradiometer and 16 per magnetometer) and the manufacturer's typical white noise of the TRIUX system, 3.5 fT/√Hz and 3.6 fT/(cm √Hz) (20.7 fT and 21.3 fT/cm in the band), with the 18-mm coil-to-helmet spacing. These three values are the typical values of the TRIUX datasheet (Elekta Neuromag, n.d.; S7): a representative Neuromag system, not one installation. Noise from the measured empty-room spectrum (25.9 fT, 20.0 fT/cm) is a sensitivity analysis. No signal-space separation is applied.

Bands. In the band sensitivity analysis (1–10, 8–30 and 30–80 Hz) the brain scale and the room field were refitted in each band, with and without a first-order 100-Hz OPM response. The dense ratio with sensor plus brain noise is 1.15 at 1–10 Hz, 1.14 at 8–30 Hz and 1.11 at 30–80 Hz (1.07 with the OPM response), against 1.14 in the analysis band; the site-matched ratio is 1.00, 1.01 and 0.99. Sensor noise is 1.3% of a dense-array channel's variance at 1–10 Hz and 23% at 30–80 Hz, and 9.3% and 74% of a Neuromag channel's. A 1–40 Hz band was chosen because the matched filter's gain rests on the lower frequencies, where the brain noise sits. In a 3–70 Hz band, typical of clinical spike review, the dense ratio with sensor plus brain noise is 1.137 (1.130 with the OPM response) and the site-matched ratio 1.011 (target-bootstrap intervals [1.133, 1.141] and [1.009, 1.013], narrower than the parcel bootstrap of the primary comparison; section A), against 1.144 and 1.009 in the analysis band; with the room field projected out they are 1.108 and 0.945. Sensor noise is 5.6% of a dense-array channel's variance and 34% of a Neuromag channel's in that band.

C.2 What is fitted, calibrated, checked and predicted

Four kinds of agreement must be kept apart (Figure S2). For the magnetometers the room field was fitted to the empty-room recording: with the manufacturer's sensor noise it reproduces their empty-room level (114.9 against 115.2 fT measured) and explains 94% of their empty-room variance. For the gradiometers the room field explains only 1.6% of that variance, so their empty-room agreement (21.4 against 20.2 fT/cm measured) is an independent check of the model's sensor-noise term, the datasheet's typical value, against this one recording, whose level it slightly exceeds. The gradiometer brain noise is calibrated: 37.1 fT/cm, equal to the measurement by construction. The magnetometer brain noise is the one independent prediction of the background at the level of a median, and it falls short: 192 fT modelled against 262 fT measured (0.73 times). The measured value is the excess of the task baseline over the empty room, which also holds non-cortical physiological fields that the model omits by design, so the shortfall is not purely an error of the cortical background; section C.4 separates the heart's part. Nothing about the OPM noise is measured: its sensor noise is declared, and its brain and room noise are the Neuromag-calibrated model seen through the OPM arrays' own lead fields.

Two published simulation studies check shape and level (S2). The depth-orientation maps of Hunold et al. (2016), adapted to this anatomy, correlate with the published dipole maps at r = 0.96 to 0.98 but reach only 0.74 to 0.79 times their level (patch maps: r = 0.93 to 0.94, 0.79 to 0.85 times), even with a background scale fitted to the paper's example traces. The calibration rule of Goldenholz et al. (2009) gives a noise-source amplitude within their published range once normalized to their number of sources (1.77 against 1.6 to 1.9 nAm; 2.68 nAm on our grid). Each adaptation keeps its paper's design rather than this study's model: Hunold et al.'s 600-nAm spike-wave sources over a background of randomly placed cortical dipoles, conductivities of 0.33, 0.0042, 0.33 S/m and their peak-to-peak SNR, and Goldenholz et al.'s 10-nAm dipoles, channel-averaged power SNR and a cortical background calibrated on recorded noise. Both adaptations already show the dependence on metric that the main text examines. With brain noise only, a site-matched OPM array's best single channel in our adaptation of Hunold's dipole maps reaches only 0.70 to 0.84 times the SNR of the planar gradiometers in every depth row, and 0.89 to 1.37 times that of the magnetometers, falling with depth, whereas with Goldenholz's channel-averaged SNR the OPM is within about 0.1 dB of both (point estimates: −0.08 dB against the gradiometers and +0.09 dB against the magnetometers).

The noise budget. In this model brain noise dominates both kinds of magnetometer channel: it is 92% of a dense-array OPM channel's in-band variance (room field 4.9%, sensor noise 3.0%) and 74% of a Neuromag magnetometer's (room field 25%, sensor noise 0.9%), whereas sensor noise is 25% of a gradiometer's. In RMS terms a dense-array channel carries 494 fT of brain noise, 114 fT of room field and 89 fT of sensor noise in the band, against 192, 113 and 21 fT at the magnetometers. The implied single-channel noise ratio OPM/magnetometer, the analogue of η, is about 2.6 in the 1 to 40 Hz band with sensor plus brain noise, the primary condition (2.3 with the room field added), against about 4.6 in the preprint's recording over a much wider band (4 to 330 Hz). Approximate readings of the preprint's spectra, not stored results, locate the difference: where brain activity dominates, the OPM/SQUID ratio of the spectral peaks is about 2.0, somewhat below the model's 2.6, whereas the measured OPM has an empty-room floor of about 28 to 35 fT/√Hz and a distance-independent low-frequency component of about 200 to 224 fT/√Hz at its peak. On this reading the model lacks the OPM's own floor and a non-brain low-frequency component, which would explain why the measured OPM noise barely fell when the sensors were lifted. The ratio of the spectral peaks and the floor are readings of the preprint's Fig. 9 with the uncertainty stated in the register (S7); the preprint is still a preprint (version 1), so these readings keep their status.

C.3 Why the ratio falls with depth, and the other adult conditions

Against Neuromag's 306 channels the fall of the dense ratio with depth is already present with sensor noise alone, from 1.00 to 0.64, because Neuromag's sensor-noise-only detectability rests on its magnetometers, and the peak-field ratio OPM/magnetometer falls from 5.41 in the shallowest bin to 2.28 at 55–60 mm; the detectability ratio falls with it, though less steeply, since the whole-array measure draws on the field at many channels. Against the 204 gradiometers alone the sensor-noise-only ratio barely changes with depth (3.56 at 10–15 mm, 3.08 at 20–25 mm and 3.44 at 60–65 mm), yet with brain noise it falls from 1.88 to 1.17 at 35–40 mm: there the depth dependence comes from brain noise, which costs every array most for deep sources. Against all 306 channels, adding brain noise multiplies the dense array's ratio by a nearly constant factor in every depth bin (1.51 to 1.68), whereas for the site-matched array the factor rises with depth (1.69 to 2.09) and partly offsets the geometric fall. The size of the factor follows the per-channel noise budget rather than better whitening by the denser array: brain noise multiplies the in-band noise of a Neuromag magnetometer about 9.3-fold over its sensor floor but that of an OPM only about 5.7-fold (a ratio of 1.65), and the sparser site-matched array has the larger factor.

Where the dense array is not ahead. With sensor plus brain noise the dense array is behind Neuromag at 4 of the 7,661 targets. They lie on the left medial orbitofrontal (3) and left medial wall (1), 26.5 to 35.1 mm below the scalp, with ratios of 0.956 to 0.997 (results/g2/g2_targets_metrics.csv gives every target's detectabilities at full precision, with an explicit flag where the OPM is ahead). Pooled over both hemispheres, 12 atlas parcels have a smaller dense lead than the parahippocampal cortex (1.12): posterior cingulate (1.03), precuneus (1.05), caudal anterior cingulate (1.05), transverse temporal (1.06), lingual (1.06), pericalcarine (1.06), paracentral (1.07), rostral anterior cingulate (1.07), isthmus cingulate (1.07), insula (1.09), medial orbitofrontal (1.12) and banks of the superior temporal sulcus (1.12).

Plug-in covariance. A covariance estimated from 780 samples costs Neuromag more than the OPMs (15% against 9.1% for the dense array and 5.1% for the site-matched array, of the oracle detectability), raising the dense ratio to 1.23 and the site-matched ratio to 1.12. With 4,680 samples, the declared 60-s scenario, the penalty is small for every array (Figure 2).

OPM noise and the site-matched array. The site-matched array's interval lies above 1 at 10 fT/√Hz (1.049 [1.032, 1.068]) and below 1 at 20 fT/√Hz (0.983 [0.967, 0.997]).

Triaxial control. At the 98 site-matched sites, three axes with the same noise on every axis (294 channels) raise the ratio from 1.01 to 1.12 (1.17 projected), close to the dense array's 1.14 from 208 single-axis sites, and to 1.05 if the tangential axes are twice as noisy.

C.4 The model against the measured Neuromag covariance

Data and comparison. The measured brain noise is the covariance of the sample recording's 320 pre-stimulus windows in the 1 to 40 Hz band minus that of the empty-room recording, with the bad channel (MEG 2443) left out and no signal-space projection, as in the adult comparison; 305 channels remain (102 magnetometers, 203 gradiometers). Two quantities of the model are fitted to this dataset: the one scalar of the cortical background (the median good-gradiometer brain variance) and the room field's 8 × 8 coefficient covariance (the empty room). Everything else is compared as an independent prediction: the magnetometers' brain-noise amplitude and channel pattern, the gradiometer level at held-out sites, the spatial correlations against inter-sensor distance, the eigenvalue spectra and dominant subspaces, the sub-band spectra, and Neuromag's detectability with the measured covariance. Where a sample covariance is compared with an exact one, the comparison is calibrated on surrogates: an exact model "measured" the same way (phase-randomized records recoloured to the model, with the same number of samples) gives the range that an exact model would show, so a value outside that range is a real departure of the measured noise from the model.

Amplitude and held-out sites. The model predicts the magnetometers' median brain-noise amplitude at 0.73 of the measured; an exact model would give 0.98 to 1.03. For the gradiometers, the scale fitted on half the sites predicts the other half's median amplitude to 1.00 [0.93, 1.09] over 1,000 random halves, but to 1.34 when fitted on the inferior half and predicting the superior half, and 0.75 the other way round: the model's spatial distribution of brain noise is too flat from bottom to top.

Channel pattern. The per-channel pattern is poorly predicted. The Pearson correlation of the log variances, model against measured, is 0.14 for the magnetometers and 0.43 for the gradiometers, against a split-half reliability of the measurement of 0.93 and 0.97. The per-channel model/measured variance spans 0.13 to 0.78 (magnetometers, 5th to 95th percentile) and 0.19 to 1.90 (gradiometers), where an exact model would give about 0.95 to 1.06. The correlation pattern between channels agrees better: the measured and modelled correlation matrices correlate at r = 0.88 (magnetometers) and 0.76 (gradiometers).

Spatial structure. (Figure S3) Against inter-sensor distance, the modelled magnetometer correlations follow the measured ones at 25–50 mm (0.85 against 0.90) and fall faster beyond (0.59 against 0.74 at 50–75 mm): at 100–125 mm the model gives 0.20 against 0.46 measured, and at 200–225 mm −0.30 against 0.00. Spatially correlated backgrounds (correlation lengths of 5 and 10 mm) do not change this, because the measured long-range correlation is a far-field pattern, not a cortical one. The gradiometers' median signed correlations agree at every distance (0.36 against 0.34 at 25–50 mm). The measured magnetometer brain noise is more concentrated than the model's: its leading five components hold 82% of the variance against 72% in the model (participation ratio 5.5 against 7.4), whereas the gradiometer spectra agree (49% against 48%). The magnetometers' excess over the model is concentrated: its leading three components hold 91% of it, and 91% of the leading component lies in the 8-dimensional far-field subspace of the room-field terms (a random direction: 7.8%).

The heart. A cardiac-locked average over 282 beats (heart rate 62 per minute) adds 127 fT RMS to the median magnetometer's baseline windows: 25% of the measured magnetometer brain variance and 51% of the model's shortfall. Its leading component lies 91% in the far-field subspace. With the heart's field removed the magnetometer prediction is 0.89 of the measured amplitude, and the correlation of the log variances rises to 0.76 (magnetometers) and 0.66 (gradiometers). The heart therefore explains about half of the shortfall and much of the pattern error; the rest is concentrated in far-field components, consistent with distant or non-cortical sources, or with cortical activity correlated over longer distances than the 5 and 10 mm tested.

Sub-bands. With the background scale refitted per band on the gradiometers, the magnetometer prediction falls with frequency: 0.97 at 1–4 Hz, 0.93 at 4–8 Hz, 0.85 at 8–13 Hz, 0.72 at 13–20 Hz, 0.57 at 20–30 Hz and 0.33 at 30–40 Hz (Figure S4). The measured magnetometer excess over the gradiometer-calibrated model grows from 1.07 to 9.28 in variance over the same bands. The band variances close on the whole band: the six sub-bands sum to 0.98 (magnetometers) and 0.95 (gradiometers) of the 1 to 40 Hz baseline variance. The magnetometers' empty-room level is predicted per band to 0.75 at 1–4 Hz and 0.98 to 1.01 from 8–13 Hz up.

Neuromag's detectability with its measured covariance. Detectability with a sample covariance is biased upward by the finite number of samples; the bias, computed on the surrogates, is 1.03 for the 305 channels, and every ratio below is divided by it target by target. Uncorrected, Neuromag's detectability with the measured covariance is 1.18 [1.12, 1.24] times its modelled value; corrected, 1.14 [1.08, 1.20], where an exact model measured the same way gives about 1. The magnetometers alone give 1.059 [1.003, 1.119] and the gradiometers 1.12 [1.07, 1.18]. The heart's field removed, the ratio is 1.15; with the measured empty room and the modelled brain noise it is 1.01, so the departure comes from the brain-noise part, not from the room. By lobe, measured over model: cingulate 1.40, frontal 1.25, insula 1.06, occipital 1.07, parietal 1.19, temporal 0.89. By depth the median ratio is 1.19 at 10–15 mm, lowest (1.09) at 40–45 mm and highest (1.42) at 60–65 mm. The real noise is thus more favourable to Neuromag than the modelled noise, most for deep and cingulate sources, because the measured excess is concentrated in a few far-field components that the whitening removes at little cost.

C.5 Scenarios A to E: what the check implies for the OPM

The OPM's covariance cannot be measured here. The implied OPM/Neuromag ratio with sensor, brain and room noise (like for like with the measured covariance, which holds the room field; Neuromag's 305 good channels) was therefore computed under five stated assumptions about the OPM's real noise:

  • Scenario A, the model's error common to both systems: the OPM's real covariance changes its detectability by the same factor, target by target, as the measured covariance changes Neuromag's. The ratio is the model's own.
  • Scenario B, both systems' cortical backgrounds scaled by the measured magnetometer variance excess k = 1.86, the model's structure kept.
  • Scenario C, the most pessimistic: Neuromag with its measured covariance, the OPM bearing the whole magnetometer variance excess as cortical noise and none of its measured structure.
  • Scenario D, Neuromag with its measured covariance, the OPM exactly as modelled (the model's error is Neuromag-specific).
  • Scenario E, Neuromag with its measured empty room and the modelled brain noise, the OPM as modelled (the sensor and room part of Neuromag's noise alone).

D. Anatomies and the children's MRI check

D.1 The adult and the three classes of smaller head

The adult is the MNE sample subject (Gramfort et al., 2013), with FreeSurfer surfaces and atlas labels (Fischl, 2012; Desikan et al., 2006) and a three-layer boundary-element model whose outer surface lies on the MRI scalp. Its usable cortex is 1,878 cm². The smaller heads belong to three classes that are not independent subjects.

Scaled adults. The two scaled adults are copies of the adult scaled by one factor (size only): the school-age size control at 0.895 times the adult, and the 2-year size control at the head circumference of the 24-month template. They keep the adult's vertices, so Δ is vertex-wise.

Infant templates. The three infant templates at 24, 18 and 12 months (O'Reilly et al., 2021) average many MRIs of the Neurodevelopmental MRI Database (Richards et al., 2016) and have smooth cortices: their usable cortex is 1,062, 975 and 895 cm², 48% to 57% of the adult's, which lowers their total background power and their patch cancellation. They were used at their native size with their own three-layer BEMs, oct-6 source spaces, atlas labels and fiducials.

School-aged children. The three individual children A, B and C (7.8 to 8.7 years; subjects sub-Z213, sub-Z209 and sub-Z226) are typically developing children of the OpenNeuro dataset ds005234 (Fadeev et al., 2024, 2025; de-identified, data licence CC0), with their own MRIs processed by the dataset's authors. Their white and sphere surfaces, atlas annotations and dense MRI scalp were used as distributed; they share each child's volume information. The dataset's boundary-element surfaces were taken from the file tree as it is distributed, in which each subject's watershed surfaces sit in the preceding subject's folder; the files were matched to each child by the volume information they share with the child's own surfaces, and the storage objects behind them carry the name of the subject they belong to. These watershed surfaces put the inner skull implausibly close to the scalp, a median 0.8 to 2.5 mm below it over the upper head, with an outer skull crossing it. The children's skull was therefore modelled: the inner skull was moved inward to 8 mm below the scalp wherever the dataset's lay shallower, with no vertex within 2 mm of a white-surface vertex (the surface between vertices comes within 0.9 to 1.3 mm of the white surface; no white vertex lies outside it), and the outer skull was put about halfway between it and the scalp. Over the upper head the modelled inner skull lies a median 5.7 to 7.8 mm below the scalp. On the adult, whose segmented skull is known, the same procedure applied to a degraded inner skull changes the primary ratio by at most 0.003 over the modelled skull depths. The head surface of the forward model is the watershed outer skin put on the MRI scalp, as for every head.

No fiducials come with the dataset. The children's fiducials, which define the head frame and so the centred placement, the origin of every scaled helmet and the OPM coverage region, were transferred from the adult by a similarity fit of the cortices and moved to the nearest scalp vertex. On the templates, which have their own fiducials, this transfer reproduces them to within 1.6 to 14.4 mm and their head frames to within 3.2 to 5.9°; the largest error, 14.4 mm, is at the 18-month template's nasion, which lies farthest from the cortex. Frame rotations of this size (total angles, their axis not resolved) are of the order of the placement family's rotations (pitch ±10°, yaw ±10°, roll ±5°; section E.1), which indicates rather than bounds their effect on the children's placements; the transferred fiducials were not checked on the children's own MRIs. Fiducials from MNI coordinates were not used: on the adult they land 10.0 to 27.4 mm from the digitized ones.

Conductivities. Every head uses the adult's conductivities (0.3, 0.006 and 0.3 S/m). With a single-compartment BEM (0.3 S/m inside the inner skull), which needs no skull or scalp conductivity, the difference of medians from the adult is +0.19 to +1.13 dB, against +0.19 to +1.14 dB with three layers (S4); across all smaller heads that difference changes by −0.06 to +0.01 dB.

Thin skull of the youngest template. As distributed, the 12-month template's skull is 0.25 mm thick at its thinnest (23 of 2,562 inner-skull vertices lie within 1 mm of the outer skull). The single-compartment BEM has no skull and is unaffected. With it the template's D is +2.03 dB against +2.04 dB with three layers, and its difference from the adult +1.06 against +1.05 dB, so the thin skull does not carry the template's result.

D.2 Regions

Regions are the lobes of the Desikan–Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006) as grouped by the study: frontal (superior, rostral middle and caudal middle frontal, pars opercularis, triangularis and orbitalis, lateral and medial orbitofrontal, precentral, paracentral and frontal pole); parietal (superior and inferior parietal, supramarginal, postcentral and precuneus); temporal (superior, middle and inferior temporal, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, fusiform, transverse temporal, entorhinal, temporal pole and parahippocampal); occipital (lateral occipital, lingual, cuneus and pericalcarine); cingulate (rostral and caudal anterior, posterior and isthmus); and insula. The medial wall belongs to none. Single parcels that the epilepsy literature emphasizes are reported as well: precentral, superior temporal ('lateral temporal' in the figures) and parahippocampal, with a mesial temporal group of the parahippocampal and entorhinal parcels; 'mesial temporal' always means this group. Both hemispheres are pooled. All sources lie on the cortical surface; hippocampus and amygdala are not modelled, so the parahippocampal and entorhinal cortex, 26.8 to 28.7 mm below the scalp in the adult (median over targets), are the nearest proxies for mesial temporal generators. The regions were chosen from the literature, and rankings among them are exploratory.

D.3 The MRI quality check of the school-aged children and the infant templates

Why it was needed. The children's white surfaces come within 3.7 to 5.7 mm of the scalp surface used for sensor placement and source depths: child B has 247 cortical targets shallower than 10 mm, the adult none. Child B's head is the size of the 24-month template's (head circumference 48.6 against 49.5 cm; cap volume 1,795 against 1,802 cm³) while its cortex is much larger (1,666 against 1,062 cm²). Both pointed to a scalp surface lying inside the true scalp, or misregistered relative to the white surface. The check was run on the three children with the adult and the 24-month template as references, using the surfaces exactly as the forward models use them, and then in the same way on the 18- and 12-month templates (results/g3b_templates_qc/children_qc.json).

What was measured. Along the outward normal of every vertex of the dense scalp, the T1 intensity was sampled from −20 to 20 mm along the normal in 0.25-mm steps. The MRI head boundary is where the profile falls through the volume's air/tissue threshold (Otsu's method; the primary edge), with the half-maximum edge and the steepest gradient as secondary definitions. The signed offset of this boundary from the scalp used, positive outward, was summarized over the cap (the head above the plane of the fiducials) by its median, quartiles and 5th to 95th percentiles, and decomposed into a uniform offset, a translation and a rotation. The frames were checked by comparing the volume geometry of every surface with the T1 header. The white surface's registration to the T1 was checked by the white/grey contrast across it: the rigid shift that maximizes the contrast was searched coarsely and finely, and the contrast gain of the best shift is reported. The head circumference was measured on the MRI head boundary and on the scalp used. Targets below 8 and 10 mm were counted against both surfaces.

Verdict rules. A head is misregistered if a surface's header differs from the T1 by more than the header tolerance, or if the white surface's realigning shift, edge translation or rotation displacement exceeds 1 mm. It is inside or outside if the cap median of the boundary's offset exceeds or falls short of the adult's by more than 1 mm (one voxel) at both the Otsu and the half-maximum edge. Otherwise it is usable.

Tests on the adult. A declared inward displacement of the adult's scalp by 4 mm was recovered as 4.0 mm, and a 2-mm translation as 2.03 mm (97% of the offset pattern explained by a translation). A 2-mm shift of the adult's white surface raises the T1 contrast at the surface by 65% when the shift is undone, so a misregistered white surface would be found.

Results (Figure S6). Every surface's header matches its T1 (corner displacements at most 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ mm in the children). The children's white surfaces are registered: the best realigning shift is 0.0 mm, the edge translation at most 0.01 mm and the rotation displacement at most 0.02 mm, and the contrast gain is 0%, 0% and 0% for children A to C. The scalp used lies a median 1.3 to 1.5 mm inside the MRI head boundary in the children at the Otsu edge (0.4 to 1.2 mm at the half-maximum edge), against 1.3 mm and 1.1 mm in the adult: a FreeSurfer scalp sits just inside the T1 edge in every individual head, the adult's included. The 24-month template's scalp lies 1.6 mm outside its boundary (verdict: outside), which is consistent with an average template whose scalp is smoothed outward; the 18- and 12-month templates' scalps lie 1.6 and 1.3 mm outside theirs (2.3 and 1.6 mm at the half-maximum edge, the 24-month template's 1.6 mm). The 12-month template's verdict is outside, the 18-month template's misregistered: its white surface fits the T1's white/grey contrast best after a rigid shift of 2.3 mm, which raises the contrast by 41%, against 0.5 mm in the other two templates, and the T1 edge across it shows a translation of 1.7 mm, both above the 1-mm tolerance. Whether this reflects a displaced surface or the weaker contrast of an average image was not settled; no template surface was repaired, and an offset of that size was not propagated to the template's results. The head circumference on the MRI boundary minus that on the scalp used is +8.9 to +10.7 mm in the children (49.7 to 54.5 against 48.6 to 53.5 cm) and +9.9 mm in the adult (59.6 against 58.6 cm). Measured from the MRI boundary instead of the scalp used, the median target depth changes by +1.3 to +1.5 mm in the children, +1.3 mm in the adult and −1.7 to −1.4 mm in the templates, whose scalps lie outside their boundaries: the children's medians shift as the adult's does, but the shallowest targets do not (below).

The shallow cortex is not a scalp-placement or registration artefact. The closest white-surface vertex lies 3.7, 3.7 and 5.7 mm below the scalp used in children A to C (4.6 to 7.5 mm below the MRI boundary), against 9.7 mm in the adult and 9.2 mm in the 24-month template, and the 5th percentile of the white surface's depth is 7.9 to 12.0 mm in the children against 14.5 mm in the adult and 11.8 mm in that template (a comparison with the adult; no pediatric norm is applied). In no child does the T1 head mask reach a face of the volume other than the inferior one, which it reaches at the neck in children B and C as in the adult, so the field of view does not cut the head above the neck. Within 8 mm of the scalp used lie 22.4, 83.4 and 3.6 cm² of white surface in children A to C, and within 8 mm of the MRI boundary 6.8, 28.8 and 0.1 cm² (adult: none). Of the cortical targets, 3, 18 and 0 lie below 8 mm and 43, 247 and 11 below 10 mm (the adult: 0; counted from the full-precision depths). Verdicts: child A usable, child B usable, child C usable (3 of 3 usable); no surface was repaired and no analysis needed rerunning. The children's shallow cortex, and child B's small head around a near-adult cortex, are not explained by their scalp surfaces or registration. One explanation the data leave open is a head boundary that the T1 images themselves place too deep: it would bring the FreeSurfer scalp, which follows the image's edge, and the dataset's watershed surfaces (inner skull a median 0.8 to 2.5 mm below the scalp; section D.1) toward the cortex together, and a check against the same images cannot detect it. An independent measurement of the scalp and skull over the upper head, from another sequence or modality, would test it. What the check cannot settle is whether the white surface is anatomically right within a few millimetres of the scalp, where the modelled scalp-and-skull layer is thinner than the adult's, nor whether three children of one dataset represent their age. The verdicts test registration and the scalp's position against the T1; they do not test anatomical plausibility, such as tissue thicknesses against norms or head circumference against centiles for age. Measured to the MRI head boundary instead of the scalp used, child B's targets less than 10 mm deep fall from 247 to 47. The source rule (no source within 4 mm of the inner skull) leaves out 7.3%, 10.0% and 3.3% of the white-surface area of children A, B and C, against 7.2% in the adult (the 7.1% of section C.6 plus the little cortex outside the inner skull; 0.05 cm² of it within 10 mm of the scalp), 9.3% to 10.6% in the scaled adults and 0.8% to 2.6% in the templates. In the children it includes 94% to 100% of the cortex within 8 mm of the scalp: of child B's 14,761 white-surface vertices there, 570 pass the source rule (results/g3b/g3b_cortex_exclusion.json). For these reasons the main text treats the children's results as provisional geometric examples.

E. Helmet fit: all constructions

E.1 The fixed adult helmet and its placements

The same Neuromag sensors and noise were used for every head. The centred placement puts each head's own head frame where the adult's lies in the sample recording (the recording's device-to-head transform), so that the ear line stays where the adult's was. The primary placement is top contact: the head is raised from the centred pose until the nearest magnetometer coil centre is 20 mm from the scalp (the 18-mm Dewar spacing plus a margin). This is a best-fit assumption rather than documented practice: a child in an adult helmet may be placed centrally and repositioned towards the expected source (Gaetz et al., 2008), and a small child's shoulders can stop the head short of the top (Tesan et al., 2010), so top contact favours Neuromag; the other placements bound this choice. A family of 12 placements chosen from the geometry alone tests placement sensitivity (11 for child C, for whom one lateral shift was infeasible): top contact, back contact, shifts of ±5 mm left–right and front–back, and rotations of ±10° pitch, ±5° roll and ±10° yaw, each raised to the same contact. Further placements are the centred pose itself, a laterally centred pose raised to contact (the head moved sideways to the helmet's midline before the raise), and true contact at the Dewar spacing.

The adult's own D depends on the placement: +1.27 dB at its measured pose, +1.00 dB at top contact, +1.01 dB laterally centred at contact and +0.87 dB at true contact. OPM arrays and noise. The OPM arrays were refitted to each head with the adult's rules: 144 to 174 dense and 80 to 91 site-matched sites on the smaller heads, at a median standoff of 6.99 to 7.00 mm above every head's own MRI scalp. They are the same in every helmet. Sensor noise, room field and band were unchanged, and the cortical background variance per unit cortical area was calibrated once on the adult, so a smaller cortex carries less total background (multiplied by 0.5 and by 2 as a sensitivity analysis).

Four candidate routes. In the realistic heads, fit and head size can act on D through at least four routes: a wider SQUID gap and a shallower cortex would raise it, while fewer OPM sites and, in the school-aged children, a near-adult cortex close to the OPMs would lower it. The constructions below vary these together in different combinations; none isolates one route (main text, Section 3.5), and the last route rests on the children's unverified near-scalp anatomy (section D.3).

E.2 The helmet scaled with the head

Every coil centre is scaled about the head origin by the child-to-adult ratio of head circumferences, keeping coil size, orientation and noise, with the factor raised in steps until no coil is within 18 mm of the scalp. The helmet is thus scaled in proportion to the head, gaps included, unlike the sphere's constant gap. It was computed about the centred head and, as a variant added after the first pediatric results to remove the templates' lateral offset, about the laterally centred head. It is a geometric construction that asks what the adult's fit would give at a child's size, not a model of a pediatric SQUID device. In the main text it is a secondary construction, because it also brings the sensors closer than the adult's own fit (below).

In the helmet scaled about the laterally centred head, Δ is −0.76 to +0.04 dB against the adult in its own scaled helmet. The upper interval bounds reach +0.20 dB, child C's (parcel-bootstrap intervals, which exclude placement and construction uncertainty). Only child C, whose scaled helmet keeps a median gap of 33.1 mm against the adult's 29.6 mm, is not negative (+0.04 [−0.20, +0.20] dB, an interval that also contains its fixed-helmet Δ). With the room field projected out, the difference of medians from the adult in its own scaled helmet is −0.92 to −0.10 dB (−0.67 to +0.05 dB with sensor plus brain noise). Scaled about the centred head instead, the helmet leaves residual gains in the heads that sit off-centre, the 24- and 18-month templates and child C (+0.24, +0.18 and +0.56 dB; Δ −0.74 to +0.56 dB overall). The OPM's absolute advantage remains in the scaled helmet (D +0.62 to +1.33 dB in the smaller heads): the additional child–adult contrast is no longer established.

Three checks qualify this result. First, part of the drop comes from the reference: against the adult at top contact rather than in its own scaled helmet, the difference of medians is −0.38 to +0.33 dB (child C +0.33 dB, above its fixed-helmet Δ). Second, scaling the helmet also scales the gap, which shrinks below the adult's 29.6 mm in 7 of the 8 smaller heads (25.1 to 28.0 mm). This helmet therefore fits most smaller heads more tightly than the adult's helmet fits the adult, which favours Neuromag; the helmet fitted at the adult's gap (section E.3) avoids this confound. Third, the smaller heads carry fewer OPM sites (144 to 174 against 208): the adult's array subsampled to those counts loses 0.27 to 0.53 dB, so at an equal OPM site count Δ in the fixed helmet would be +0.43 to +1.43 dB.

E.4 Depth matching

Depth is the route of head size that the sphere describes. The templates' and children's cortex is shallower than the adult's: area-weighted median depths of 20.6 to 23.2 mm (templates) and 20.0 to 23.4 mm (children) against 26.2 mm, with 36% to 46% and 35% to 47% of the cortical area 10–20 mm below the scalp against 21% in the adult. In the fixed helmet, reweighting each head's targets to the adult's depth distribution lowers the difference of pooled medians from the adult: templates, pooled +0.85 to +1.05 dB, reweighted +0.29 to +0.53 dB; children, pooled +0.19 to +0.52 dB, reweighted −0.05 to +0.05 dB. Within depth strata, where Δ is the difference of the two heads' area-weighted medians, it is positive in every stratum of the scaled adults and templates (+0.11 to +1.62 dB; Figure S7). For the individual children it is negative in 11 of the 12 child-strata of 10–30 mm depth (−0.36 to +0.08 dB; no interval lies above zero, the largest lower bound being −0.25 dB) and positive deeper (+0.01 to +1.77 dB). In this decomposition, with depths measured from the scalp used (unverified near the scalp in the children, and shifted by +1.3 to +1.5 mm in the children, +1.3 mm in the adult and −1.7 to −1.4 mm in the templates if measured from the MRI boundary; section D.3), the children's fixed-helmet gain is matched by their shallower cortex; this describes the model's surfaces, not a tested mechanism. In the helmet fitted at the adult's gap the children's strata are negative throughout the depths that hold most of their cortex: for child A, Δ at the adult's gap is −1.70 dB at 10–15 mm, −0.91 dB at 20–25 mm and −0.20 dB at 30–40 mm, and its intervals include zero only from the 40–50 mm stratum down. The two decompositions, fitting the helmet and matching depth, are not additive, and neither apportions the routes exactly; the fitted helmet is the main control because it matches the adult's median gap, although it changes the gap distribution, the placement and the coverage with it.

E.5 The noise in the smaller heads, and the share of cortex where the OPM is ahead

The noise differs between the classes under the convention that the background variance per unit cortical area is the adult's. In the fixed helmet, Neuromag's magnetometer brain noise falls from 202 fT in the adult at top contact (192 fT at its measured pose) to 98 to 150 fT in the templates and scaled adults, with both their wider gap and their smaller cortex contributing (their shares are not separated): moving the school-age size control from its centred pose up to top contact raises it from 131 to 150 fT. A smaller cortex with the same background per unit area does not lower the field at sensors that stay close, as the OPMs show (525 and 537 fT in the scaled adults against 494 fT in the adult). The children's cortex is nearly adult-sized (1,666 to 1,869 cm²): Neuromag's brain noise stays at 168 to 194 fT, and the OPMs, close to a near-adult cortex that lies near the scalp in these surfaces (unverified there; section D.3), see more of it (617 to 751 fT).

The share of cortex on which the OPM is ahead, the preprint's kind of metric, is saturated for the dense array (99.6% of cortical area in the adult). For the site-matched array it is 49% in the adult at top contact (against 56% of all targets at the measured pose) and 58% to 89% in the smaller heads in the fixed helmet (Figure S8c).

F.2 The noise floor across heads

D falls with the OPM's sensor noise in every head (Figure S11). G. The spike study: design, decisions and localization detail

G.1 The exploratory design

Time-domain model. The detection study runs the noise model in the time domain: background moments with a 1/f spectrum, a room field with the cross-spectra of the empty-room recording and white sensor noise, each scaled so that its in-band covariance equals that of the adult model (the room field is not projected out), filtered to 1–40 Hz and decimated to 150.2 Hz, with one realization per 30-s segment shared by all arrays (Table S17). The spike is the spike-wave complex of Hunold et al. (2016), digitized, stretched in time by 0.75, 1 and 1.5 (3 morphologies) and passed through the same filter.

Events. Spikes were placed at 72 locations stratified by depth below the scalp (10–20, 20–30, 30–45 and 45–70 mm; 18 locations per band) and by orientation (3 bands from radial to tangential; 6 locations per depth and orientation stratum). Each location received 18 focal-dipole events (6 strengths of 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 and 320 nAm, each with every morphology) and patch events of 10 mm radius carrying the same total moments with the unstretched morphology: 1,296 focal and 432 patch events, 1,728 per anatomy, injected in random order one every 2 s, identically in every array. The adult's locations lie 24 in the frontal lobe, 14 parietal, 14 temporal, 12 cingulate, 6 insula and 2 occipital, at median depths of 16.9, 26.0, 36.8 and 51.7 mm in the four bands.

Detectors. Every array was read by two detectors, and Neuromag also by its magnetometers and its gradiometers alone (5 sensor sets per anatomy, each read by both detectors). Both detectors whiten the data with a Ledoit–Wolf covariance from 10 min of null data and filter them with the spike templates. The oracle knows source and time: it tests the whitened, template-filtered projection on the true field pattern at the true peak (one-sided) against a threshold for a per-trial false-positive probability of 0.001. The practical detector knows neither: at every sample it takes the largest normalized output over the 3 templates and 441 to 716 candidate field patterns on a 10-mm cortical grid that excludes the true sources; its local maxima at least 0.25 s apart are events, and a spike counts as detected when an event above threshold lies within ±50 ms of its peak, the same events being counted as false on null data. The templates are the simulated morphologies and the candidates' field patterns come from the true forward model, which makes the practical detector idealized for every array; whether equally so was not tested in the exploratory run (the confirmatory run's mismatch variant tests one departure), and focal on-scalp patterns may be more sensitive to forward-model and coregistration error. Its thresholds for 1 false event per minute were set on 20 min of independent null data and frozen; 20 min of held-out null data gave 0.40 to 1.55 false events per minute across sensor sets and anatomies (Table S9). In the adult the practical detector needs 1.83 to 2.08 times the oracle's strength for half detection at 10–20 mm.

S50 and the tests. S50 is the strength at which the detection rate of a band's focal events (324 events: the morphologies pooled over the band's 18 locations) reaches 50%, interpolated linearly in log strength between the tested strengths; the patch events enter neither S50 nor the paired tests. The paired ratio S50Neuromag/S50OPM is above 1 when the OPM needs less strength (section A). The adult sits at its measured pose and the smaller heads at top contact; the measured pose gives the adult a D 0.28 dB higher than top contact, so the adult's spike results are not placed by the same rule as the smaller heads'.

Endpoints named after the analyses. The exploratory endpoints were (i) the dense array's detectability ratio against Neuromag's 306 channels in the adult; (ii) Δ of the dense array against the same comparator at top contact in the fixed helmet and in the helmet scaled with the head about the laterally centred head, each against the adult placed by the same rule, reported descriptively; and (iii) for the spikes, the dense array against Neuromag's 306 channels in the 10–20 mm band with the practical detector, tested with the two-sided exact sign-flip test on per-location differences in detection counts, Holm-adjusted (Holm, 1979) over the 9 anatomies, with the paired S50 ratio and its location-bootstrap interval as the effect size. This band, array and detector were chosen from the 24 comparisons per OPM array of each anatomy, a selection that the correction over anatomies does not control. The detection family has 48 comparisons per anatomy (two OPM arrays against Neuromag's 306 channels, its gradiometers and its magnetometers, with both detectors, in 4 depth bands) and the localization family 40 (two OPM arrays; focal and patch sources at two strengths; the dipole error, the two dSPM errors, and joint detection and localization with the study's dSPM or with the dipole).

What the location design allows. The sign-flip test uses the locations whose difference in detection counts is not zero, and p is smallest when all favour one array: 7.6 × 10⁻⁶ with a band's 18 locations; p < 0.05 needs at least 6 such locations. With equal differences the test is the exact sign test: of 18 locations, 14 must favour one array for p < 0.05 (p = 0.031; with 13, 0.096) and 16 for the first Holm step over 9 anatomies, p < 0.0056 (p = 0.0013); of the confirmatory 36 (12 per orientation stratum), 25 (p = 0.029) and 27 (p = 0.0039). Unequal differences can move p either way (the adult's 11 to 1: p = 0.0039; 0.0063 if equal). Beyond 20 non-zero locations the run estimated p from 20,000 random sign patterns (resolution 5 × 10⁻⁵); the p values reported for the confirmatory run are exact (section G.5). These values follow from the design alone; power, and a minimal detectable S50 ratio, depend on how detection varies between locations and were not computed. Two pilot runs on test seeds (child A, 18 locations) gave 8/1 and 9/4 locations favouring the OPM/Neuromag, p = 0.074 and 0.21; they are recorded in the declaration, where its comment describes 18 locations as underpowered, a reading the manuscript does not adopt; their outputs were not kept; they prompted doubling the locations to 36 but do not estimate power.

G.3 Localization

Design. Localization was examined on 24 locations with focal and patch sources of 80 and 320 nAm, one event per source type and strength at each location (24 per condition, unstretched morphology) in independent null data. The inverse used a single-compartment BEM (the simulation used three layers), one of 8 coregistration-error draws of 2 mm and 2° per location, the same for every array (a median displacement of 2.9 mm of the adult's sources; 2.1 to 3.2 mm over the anatomies), a Ledoit–Wolf noise covariance from 5 min of null data and a fixed-orientation source grid at 5-mm spacing (2,114 to 3,821 sources) that excludes the true source vertices. Dipoles were fitted with MNE-Python at least 5 mm inside the inner skull, and dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM; Dale et al., 2000), with depth weighting 0.8 and a regularization set by an assumed SNR of 3, was computed in the study's implementation and in MNE-Python's, at the true peak sample. The error is the distance from the true source (a patch's centre) to the dipole or to the dSPM peak, read on the MRI through the perturbed transform; joint success is detection by the practical detector (thresholds from 10 min of null data) with an error of at most 10 mm. Paired error differences were tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and joint success with an exact McNemar test.

The two dSPM implementations. Both use fixed orientations on the same grid, the same noise covariance, a depth exponent of 0.8, λ² = 1/9, a source covariance scaled to the whitened rank and dSPM normalization. They differ in the depth weighting: the study's uses the whitened gains of all of an array's channels, without a limit; MNE-Python's is limited at its default of 10 and uses the unwhitened gains of the gradiometers, or of the magnetometers where there are none (the OPM arrays). Both are reported, because the paired dSPM differences depend on this choice.

Results. Dipole fits of detected 320-nAm focal spikes have median errors of 3.7 to 7.6 mm in every array and anatomy. Of 360 paired comparisons, 65 have p < 0.05, most of them for dSPM errors (21 with the study's implementation, 27 with MNE-Python's), 6 for dipole errors and 11 for joint detection and dSPM localization within 10 mm; 18 would be expected by chance. Of these, 61 favour an OPM array, 3 have a zero median difference and 1 favours Neuromag; 28 concern mostly undetected 80-nAm sources, and the two dSPM implementations share their events. In all, 8 comparisons survive a Bonferroni correction within anatomy and array, 6 of them at 80 nAm, where most sources go undetected, and 0 survive one over all 360. The adult's dSPM gain for 320-nAm patches depends on the implementation (−5.6 mm, p = 0.054, with the study's estimator; −1.0 mm, p = 0.22, with MNE's).

Effect sizes. Figure S14 draws every paired error difference with its interval. Of the 216 error comparisons, 54 have p < 0.05 (6 for the dipole fit, 27 for MNE-Python's dSPM, 21 for the study's) and 8 fall below 0.0025, the Bonferroni threshold for the 20 comparisons of one anatomy and OPM array. At 320 nAm the dipole-fit differences lie within a few millimetres of zero in every anatomy, and the dSPM differences are negative or zero. At 80 nAm all 108 estimates rest mostly on undetected events, whose errors are those of noise, and their intervals are wide. 20 medians are exactly zero, because the dSPM peaks of both systems often fall on the same vertex of the shared 5-mm grid, and 30 are positive (Neuromag closer). The errors include every event, detected or not; selecting on detection would select on each system's own noise.

Joint detection and localization. Figure S15 gives the share of injected events that each system both detects and localizes within 10 mm. Of the 72 paired comparisons per method, 0 with the dipole fit and 11 with the study's dSPM have p < 0.05 (exact McNemar, uncorrected), all favouring the OPM array; none survives a correction. Joint success assumes the event time is known, because localization is read at the true peak sample.

Failed fits. Every event received a dipole. Under declared criteria, a dipole or dSPM error above 30 mm is gross and a dipole confidence volume above 10 cm³ unconstrained. Over the 864 events per system, Neuromag has 332 gross dipole errors (27 among its 456 detected events), the dense array 310 (44 among 501 detected) and the site-matched array 325 (38 among 475): 5.9% to 8.8% of detected events. Gross errors of the study's dSPM among detected events are 77 (Neuromag, 17%), 39 (dense, 7.8%) and 36 (site-matched, 7.6%). Of all gross errors (dipole and the study's dSPM, three arrays), 87% belong to undetected events and 73% to 80-nAm events. The denominators differ between arrays because each array detects different events, so these rates are described, not compared.

What the localization study does not show. The spike study does not test the fitted helmet, so it does not show that fit causes a detection benefit in children. No localization difference is established in any anatomy.

G.4 Head motion

Head motion and slippage of the OPM cap were bounded rather than simulated in time, on the adult and the 24- and 12-month templates at top contact: the head was displaced in the fixed helmet by up to 10 mm or rotated about the head origin, and the dense OPM cap rotated on the head by up to 3°, sensors that would enter the scalp being lifted; each case was evaluated with the matched filter of the reference geometry (uncompensated) and with the displaced geometry known (S5). A sustained 10-mm drop in the fixed helmet costs Neuromag 1.64 to 1.72 dB uncompensated and 0.36 to 0.39 dB with the displaced geometry known, the latter comparable to an uncompensated 3° slip of the OPM cap (0.12 to 0.46 dB). A 60-s time course of exact rigid motion checked the field-artefact model only (S5); motion in the spike simulations, and its interaction with detection thresholds, was not simulated.

G.5 The confirmatory run

The confirmatory design, fixed before the run, is stated in the main text (Section 2.5). Its declarations in full:

  • seeds: a root seed (20261005), from which one stream per anatomy and purpose is spawned;
  • locations: 36 newly drawn per anatomy in the 10–20 mm band (12 per orientation stratum), vertex-disjoint from the exploratory ones where a stratum allows;
  • test: the two-sided sign-flip test, exact up to 20 non-zero location differences and otherwise estimated from 20,000 random sign patterns;
  • effect size: the paired S50 ratio with a location-bootstrap interval of 1,000 resamples;
  • null data: separate segments for the whitener, for fitting the thresholds, for the held-out check and for a 20-min evaluation of the false-event rates of the frozen and the rate-matched thresholds;
  • replicates: 5 noise replicates per event, the first confirmatory and the others measuring Monte Carlo variability;
  • the mismatched detector: templates at the geometric midpoints between the injected morphologies, candidate patterns from a single-compartment BEM and a coregistration error of 2 mm and 2° shared by all arrays.

The design was declared in the repository's configuration file (configs/g4_confirmatory.toml) and committed to the repository before the run (commit 10e37b9; main text, Section 2.5; S9). Its primary result is in the main text (Section 3.6, Table 3 and Figure 8); the design as run, the Monte Carlo replicates, the realized false-event rates, the mismatch costs, the site-matched array and the results pooled over the replicates follow here.

Design as run. The run was made at one code commit (ae458a9) with the configuration as declared and is complete in all nine anatomies. Every random stream was spawned from the root seed 20261005 by anatomy and purpose (10 purposes), the noise replicates of an event from its event stream, except the random sign patterns of the sign-flip test, which use the fixed seed 0 of the test's implementation in every anatomy, family and replicate (Table S16). The 36 locations per anatomy (12 in each of the 3 orientation strata) were drawn from pools of 109 to 2,249 candidate vertices per stratum after the 72 exploratory locations had been left out, so that no stratum shares a vertex with the exploratory run: the nearest exploratory location lies 2.7 mm away at the closest and a median 13.6 to 18.6 mm away over the anatomies, and the locations' median depth is 12.9 to 17.4 mm (the adult's lie 17 in the frontal lobe, 10 parietal, 8 temporal and 1 occipital). Each location received 18 focal events per replicate (6 strengths of 10 to 320 nAm, morphologies stretched by 0.75, 1 and 1.5): 648 events per replicate, 3,240 per anatomy over the five replicates and 29,160 in all. The null data were 10 min for the whitener, 20 min for the thresholds (20 events above each frozen threshold), 20 min held out and 20 min of evaluation null, 70 min in all; the practical detector's dictionary held 452 to 728 candidate patterns over the anatomies. With more than 20 non-zero location differences the run estimated each sign-flip p from 20,000 random sign patterns (resolution 5 × 10⁻⁵), and an estimate was zero where no sampled pattern reached the observed statistic. The differences are integer counts, so the exact p over all 2n sign patterns follows from the distribution of the signed sum. It was computed from the stored differences for all 540 cells of anatomy and comparison (results/g4_confirm/g4_confirm_exact_p.json, written by scripts/g4_confirm_exact_p.py); the 47 cells that the run had enumerated itself are reproduced exactly. Every p reported here and in the main text is this exact value. The run's estimates differ from it by at most 0.0073 and give the same Holm decision for every anatomy in all 25 families; where an estimate was zero, the exact Holm-adjusted value reaches 0.00040. The S50 intervals use 1,000 location resamples. The oracle's realized per-trial false-positive probability on the held-out null is 0.00093 to 0.0011 over the anatomies and arrays, against the declared 0.001. Before the run, the confirmatory script's endpoint statistics were applied to the exploratory run's stored states: they reproduce the committed exploratory location counts, p-values and S50 ratios exactly in all 36 comparisons (nine anatomies, both OPM arrays, both detectors).

The mismatch variant. Its templates sit at the geometric midpoints of the injected stretches (0.87 and 1.22: 2 templates, each injected morphology 13% to 18% away from the nearest), its candidate patterns come from a 1-layer BEM, and one coregistration error per anatomy (2 mm, 2°), shared by all arrays, displaces the candidate dictionary by a median of 1.6 to 4.9 mm at the spike locations over the anatomies (0.7 to 5.9 mm over all locations). Its thresholds were set for a nominal 1 false event per minute on the calibration null and frozen and, like the primary detector's, re-fitted on the held-out null as a secondary analysis.

H. Analysis history

This note collects, in one place, what was decided before and after results were known, so that the reader can tell a pre-specified result from an exploratory one.

Named before any run. The analysis configuration files of the adult and pediatric studies named two noise conditions before any simulation: sensor plus brain noise, the primary one, and the room field projected out. The whole-cortex results are reported in both conditions in the main text and here, the regional results with sensor plus brain noise; the Abstract and Conclusions lead with sensor plus brain noise.

Named after the analyses. No endpoint, test or margin of the original study was declared before its analyses. The exploratory spike endpoint (the dense array against Neuromag's 306 channels at 10–20 mm with the practical detector) was chosen from 24 comparisons per OPM array (section G.1). The regions were chosen from the epilepsy literature (section D.2), and rankings among them are exploratory. The primary placement of the fixed helmet, top contact, was chosen after the adult's whole-cortex results were known, and the laterally centred placement and the helmet scaled about the laterally centred head were added after the first pediatric results, to remove the templates' lateral offset (section E.2); the 12 source-blind placements (section E.1) bound the first choice. The main text states the post hoc caveat once (Section 2.6) and reports every exploratory result as such.

Model versions. The model went through four versions before the results were frozen. The first three took the OPM sensitive axes from the adult's stored outer-skin normals, which are nearly radial, and placed the adult's head surface outside its MRI scalp (by a median of 1.1 mm, the move of the final version's conform step); the second checked the OPM cell's clearance from the head at its integration points only, and the third at the whole cell, which moved most of the adult's sites outward. The final version puts every head's surface on its MRI scalp, so that the clearance rule moves only the sites the anatomy demands and the OPM standoff is the same (6.99 to 7.00 mm) above every head's own MRI scalp (the templates' scalp convention differs from the adult's; section D.3), and takes the sensitive axes from that surface's own triangles (section C.1). This last change, equal OPM standoff on every head, was made after the pediatric results were known. It lowered Δ by 0.11 to 0.27 dB in the scaled adults and templates (the school-aged children were added afterwards and exist only in the final version), raised the adult's projected dense ratio (the earlier construction gave 1.048; the final sites with the earlier, nearly radial axes give 1.073; the final model gives 1.115), made the laterally centred scaled-helmet Δ of the scaled adults and templates negative, and strengthened the adult's exploratory spike result at 10–20 mm (earlier 8/2 locations, p = 0.037, S50 ratio 1.29, against 11/1 and 1.36 in the final version). Every array change redraws the noise, because an array's channel count changes the random stream. The dense array's advantage at 10–20 mm held in every version; which deeper cells reach p < 0.05 did not: the site-matched array's deficit at 45–70 mm in two earlier versions (0/7 locations in the second, 1/9 with p = 0.016 in the third) does not reach p < 0.05 in the final one (3/6 locations, p = 0.40). Results that depend on these choices are post hoc, the earlier result files are not part of the stored results, and persistence across versions is not replication.

Added in the revision. In the revision, analyses were added without changing the model: the check of the noise model against the measured Neuromag covariance and the sensitivity analyses (section C), the MRI quality check of the school-aged children (section D.3) and the helmet fitted at the adult's gap (section E.3). Each reproduces the stored results it builds on to within rounding, as its result file records, and each was run at one code commit named in that file. The confirmatory spike run was designed before it was run (main text, Section 2.5) and is the only pre-specified test of the study.

Parameter provenance. From the manufacturer's TRIUX datasheet: the Neuromag noise values and the 18-mm coil-to-helmet spacing (S7). From the preprint's description of its sensors and from device papers: the 7-mm standoff of the sensing centre; the 10-mm cell is a modelling choice, an averaging volume rather than a device cell, whose effect at the field peak is at most 1.9% (S7). Declared simulation choices without a device specification: the primary OPM noise of 15 fT/√Hz (the middle of the 7 to 30 fT/√Hz sweep), the 17-mm minimum OPM spacing (standing for a package of 12 to 17 mm), the 100-Hz OPM response and the 1/fx background spectrum. Figure readings of the preprint: its Fig. 8 bar values (from the 600-dpi raster, about ±2%) and its empty-room OPM floor, 29 to 31 dB re fT²/Hz on Fig. 9, or 28 to 35 fT/√Hz if the unstated reference is 1 fT²/Hz. The register (S7) lists every entry and its status.

Literature synthesis. We searched PubMed and Europe PMC for OPM studies of epilepsy (searches of 4 October 2026: optically pumped magnetometers, OPM-MEG or on-scalp MEG with epilepsy, interictal or epileptiform activity, seizures, children or simulation, and author searches), the reference lists of Feys et al. (2025), Schwartz et al. (2025) and Shen et al. (2026), and the web for preprints. For each study we extracted the design, patients, regions, OPM technology, comparator, SNR definition and results, checking each value against the full text (version of record or accepted manuscript) for 13 of the 15 studies (12 clinical and 3 modelling), against the preprint's full text for Feys et al. (2022) and against the abstract alone for Ren et al. (2025), whose full text was not read (open access, but its publisher's page could not be retrieved for this revision); Ren et al. therefore carries direction only. The model's counterparts (main text, Table 4) come from the same result files as every other number.
