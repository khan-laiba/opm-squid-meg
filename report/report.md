# Brain region, head size and helmet fit in on-scalp versus SQUID MEG: a simulation study from adults to children with interictal spikes

**Laiba Khan**

*A simulation study. Every number from the model is inserted when this page is built, read from the project's stored result files, configurations and methods notes ([S7](numbers.html) gives the source of each); literature values come from a verified extraction of the cited studies. No author of the reproduced or cited studies has reviewed or endorsed this work.*

**Abstract**

**Background.** On-scalp optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) record larger brain fields than the superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) of conventional magnetoencephalography (MEG), but a larger signal need not give a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). A spherical model predicts an OPM advantage only above an equal-SNR depth set by an assumed noise ratio (Jas et al., 2026), while clinical epilepsy studies report mixed SNR gains, weakest for temporal sources.

**Methods.** We replaced the assumed noise ratio with explicit sensor, cortical-background and room noise on a realistic adult head, comparing the {{ F.g2_squid_channels }}-channel Neuromag system with dense and site-matched OPM arrays by known-topography detectability (D, dB). We repeated this in {{ F.g4_n_smaller_heads }} smaller heads (scaled adults, infant templates, school-aged children) in the fixed adult helmet and in a helmet scaled with the head (Δ = D~child~ − D~adult~), and simulated interictal spikes.

**Results.** With {{ F.g2_opm_asd_primary }}-fT/√Hz OPMs and sensor plus brain noise, the dense array's median detectability ratio was {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci }} and the site-matched array's {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci }} (95% parcel-bootstrap intervals); Neuromag led with sensor noise alone ({{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_int_ratio }}) and in peak-channel SNR ({{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), and {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }}-fT/√Hz OPMs lost the advantage ({{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}). Among the regions examined, the site-matched array trailed most in mesial temporal cortex ({{ F.reg_mesial_temporal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}). In the fixed helmet D rose in every smaller head (Δ {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB), least robustly in the individual children; a helmet scaled with the head and laterally centred removed this extra gain (Δ {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB; {{ F.g3b_smaller_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB without lateral centring). The dense array detected spikes {{ F.g4_band_10_20mm }} mm deep at {{ F.g4_all_dense_vs_combined_practical_10_20mm_reduction_pct_range }} lower strength in every anatomy (point estimates; Holm-adjusted per-location tests, largest p {{ F.g4_holm_10_20mm_max_p_adj }}; endpoint chosen after the analyses); deeper spikes and localization showed no established difference.

**Conclusions.** In this model the OPM advantage is real but conditional on comparator, metric, array density and sensor noise, and largest for superficial sources. Smaller heads gain more in an adult helmet through a wider SQUID gap and a shallower cortex; scaling the helmet with the head removes the extra gain.

**Highlights**

- Brain and room noise modelled explicitly; OPM sensor noise declared and swept
- Dense OPM arrays lead Neuromag modestly; comparator, metric and noise decide
- In an adult helmet, smaller heads gain more through both fit and shallower cortex
- Dense OPMs detect weak superficial spikes at lower strength (point estimates)

## 1. Introduction {#intro}

Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) measure the brain's magnetic field at room temperature and can be mounted within millimetres of the scalp, whereas the superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) of conventional magnetoencephalography (MEG) sit in a rigid cryogenic helmet at a distance set by its Dewar (Boto et al., 2017; Iivanainen et al., 2017). The helmet is built for adults: a child's head sits in it with a wide and uneven gap to the sensors, whereas an on-scalp array can be placed on any head (Hill et al., 2019; Feys & De Tiège, 2024; Jas et al., 2026). Two benefits are therefore expected from OPMs: larger signals in general, and a further gain in children.

Jas et al. (2026) showed that neither benefit follows from the larger signal alone. In a spherical head with point magnetometers on the scalp (OPM) and {{ F.lit_xi_squid_mm }} mm above it (SQUID), the OPM always records the larger field; but if its noise is η times the SQUID's, its single-channel signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is higher only for sources shallower than an equal-SNR depth d~eq~, about {{ F.lit_deq_eta3_printed_mm }} mm at η = {{ F.lit_eta_reference }} in an adult. With a SQUID shell that follows the head at a constant {{ F.lit_xi_squid_mm }}-mm standoff, the same rule leaves a larger fraction of a small brain on the OPM's side (a normalized d~eq~ of {{ F.lit_norm_deq_newborn_printed_pct }} in a newborn against {{ F.lit_norm_deq_adult_printed_pct }} in an adult), which the authors read as a benefit for children. In their recording of median-nerve responses, OPM noise barely changed when the sensors were lifted from the scalp whereas SQUID noise fell, which they read as SQUID noise dominated by brain activity, and the SQUID magnetometer's SNR for the early cortical response (N20) exceeded the OPM's (about {{ F.lit_sef_squid_snr_xi0 }} against {{ F.lit_sef_opm_snr_xi0 }}, read from the preprint's figure). Their model is a single dipole in a spherically symmetric head, with radial point magnetometers, the same SQUID standoff for every head and brain noise represented only through η; the authors name anatomically realistic heads, larger OPM arrays and the dependence of brain noise on the distance to the sensors as the open questions. This study starts from those questions.

Clinical OPM studies of epilepsy bear on both expected benefits. They confirm the first for amplitude, but not uniformly for SNR (Section 4.2). Every OPM-versus-SQUID comparison of interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) that reports amplitude found it larger with OPMs, 2.3 to 4.6 times in five school-aged children (Feys et al., 2022). The SNR was higher with OPMs in four of those children against the SQUID system's 102 magnetometers (Feys et al., 2022), in one of three statistically compared patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (similar in two; Feys et al., 2025) and in a larger cohort (Ren et al., 2025; only its abstract was available to us). It was slightly lower when few OPMs were compared with fuller SQUID comparators: six dual-axis sensors against planar gradiometers (Hillebrand et al., 2023) and four helium-4 sensors against the nearest channels of a 275-channel system (Schwartz et al., 2025). These SNRs are not one quantity: a peak-channel amplitude over a baseline standard deviation (Feys et al., 2022, 2025), a per-spike maximum over a baseline standard deviation on the channels nearest the OPMs (Schwartz et al., 2025), a detector z-score (Hillebrand et al., 2023), or not stated in the abstract (Ren et al., 2025). Where regions were separated, temporal and especially mesial temporal sources gave the weakest OPM results, mostly in comparisons within OPM data (Feys et al., 2024, 2025; Shen et al., 2026) and in single patients also against the SQUID (Feys et al., 2025; Schwartz et al., 2025).

Simulation studies have compared on-scalp and SQUID arrays before, on adult heads (Boto et al., 2016; Iivanainen et al., 2017; Riaz et al., 2017) and across ages. With sensor noise only, Zahran et al. (2022) placed the heads of a 9-year-old and ten infants both in a fitted SQUID helmet (2 cm all round) and in the standard placement against the helmet top: the advantage of a three-axis helium-4 OPM array over 102 SQUID magnetometers grew as the head shrank, and an array measuring only the normal field component, below the SQUIDs in the adult, exceeded the standard-placed SQUID array in infants. With brain noise on one adult anatomy, simulated spikes became visible on scalp from smaller cortical patches than with conventional MEG or electroencephalography (EEG), mesial temporal spikes included, but the smallest seizure-onset patch was not visible on scalp in the mesial temporal network (Westin et al., 2023). With brain noise only in 54 patients of 4.7 to 61.4 years, OPMs at SQUID-matched positions raised the SNR in no region, whereas a layout targeted at the posterior fossa raised cerebellar SNR (Matsubara et al., 2026, from the group of Jas et al.). The models with brain noise thus used one anatomy or OPMs at the SQUID positions, and none separated head size from helmet fit; the model that did compare head sizes had sensor noise only.

The sphere and the clinical reports therefore seem to disagree: in one, OPMs win only for superficial sources, with a boundary set by an assumed η; in the other, gains are region-specific and sometimes reversed. They need not conflict. The clinical studies measure sensor-level SNR of real discharges against different comparators (magnetometers only, gradiometers or the few nearest channels), with very different numbers and kinds of OPMs, whereas the sphere compares one magnetometer with another under an assumed η. We therefore ask:

- **Q1.** What replaces the assumed η when sensor, brain and room noise are explicit and the geometry is a real head in a real helmet?
- **Q2.** Which brain regions favour the OPM, under which metric and comparator, and does this explain the clinical reports?
- **Q3.** Is the larger relative benefit expected in children due to head size or to the fixed adult helmet, and how does it depend on brain region and head size?
- **Q4.** Does a detectability difference change which simulated interictal spikes are detected, and how well they are localized?

Throughout, we also ask how much depends on the number of OPM sensors, a question of spatial sampling in its own right (Tierney et al., 2020), by comparing a dense array with an array that has one sensor at each Neuromag site inside the OPM coverage region, and with channel-count controls. Sections 3.1 to 3.4 answer Q1 in the adult, Section 3.5 Q2, Sections 3.6 to 3.9 Q3 and Section 3.10 Q4; Section 4.1 collects the answers and Section 4.2 sets them against the clinical studies.

The novel element is not a new sensor model but the combination: the noise ratio becomes an outcome of explicit brain and room noise for a declared OPM sensor noise, which we sweep; the pediatric contrast is decomposed in real helmet geometry with that noise; and the clinical literature is read against one model by region, comparator and metric.

## 2. Methods {#methods}

This section gives what is needed to read the results; every step, parameter and check is described in [S5](methods.html) and [S6](register.html).

### 2.1 Spherical benchmark {#meth-sphere}

The analytical model of Jas et al. (2026) was reimplemented with their definitions: a tangential current dipole of {{ F.g1a_q_nam }} nAm in a spherical conductor (head radius {{ F.g1a_h_mm }} mm, brain radius {{ F.g1a_b_mm }} mm), point magnetometers measuring the radial field on the scalp (OPM) and {{ F.g1a_xi_squid_mm }} mm above it (SQUID), single-channel SNR equal to the peak field over the noise σ, and σ~OPM~ = η σ~SQUID~. The equal-SNR depth is the exact root of the preprint's Eq. 3 (reproduction in [@fig-sphere]a). The peak field was checked against the full Sarvas (1987) field maximized over the sensor sphere (largest relative difference {{ F.g1a_eq1_vs_sarvas_rel_err_opm }}) and against MNE-Python's sphere model ({{ F.g1a_eq1_vs_mne_rel_err_opm }}). For head size, the preprint's newborn, {{ F.g3a_infant1yr_age_years }}-year, {{ F.g3a_child8yr_age_years }}-year and adult spheres were placed in a SQUID shell that follows each head at {{ F.lit_xi_squid_mm }} mm, as in the preprint, and, as a new contrast, all in the adult shell of radius {{ F.g3a_fixed_shell_radius }} mm.

### 2.2 Adult head, sensor arrays and forward model {#meth-arrays}

The adult is the MNE sample subject (Gramfort et al., 2013), whose cortical surfaces and atlas labels are FreeSurfer reconstructions (Fischl, 2012). Sources were the {{ F.g2_n_targets }} usable vertices of an oct-6 source space on the white surface (at least {{ F.g2_source_skull_clearance_mm }} mm inside the inner skull), each a {{ F.g2_focal_nam }}-nAm dipole normal to the cortex; {{ F.g2_medialwall_n }} of them lie on the medial wall, which the adult medians keep and the pediatric summaries leave out (Section 2.4). Extended sources were geodesic patches of {{ F.g2_patch_radii_mm }} mm radius around each target, the signed sum of cortex-normal dipoles with a total moment of {{ F.g2_patch_total_nam }} nAm. Depth is the distance from a target to the nearest vertex of the head's dense MRI scalp surface (not the radial depth of the sphere), and orientation the angle between the cortical normal and the local inner-skull normal. The three-layer boundary-element model (BEM; Hämäläinen & Sarvas, 1989; conductivities {{ F.g2_conductivity }} S/m) had its outer surface placed on the scalp of the magnetic resonance image (MRI) and refined to {{ F.g2_head_triangles }} triangles. The Neuromag model used the sample recording's sensor geometry (a VectorView helmet) with TRIUX coil definitions and MNE-Python's accurate coil integration, at the head position measured in that recording: {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels, {{ F.g2_squid_sites }} magnetometers and {{ F.g2_squid_grad_channels }} planar gradiometers. Its magnetometer coils were a median {{ F.g2_squid_scalp_dist_median_mm }} mm from the nearest scalp point ({{ F.g2_squid_scalp_dist_p5_p95_mm_range }} mm, 5th to 95th percentile).

The OPMs were single-axis sensors with a {{ F.g2_opm_cell_mm }}-mm cubic sensing volume, the sensing centre {{ F.g2_dense_standoff_mm }} mm above the MRI scalp and the sensitive axis along the local normal of the head surface. The coverage region is the scalp above a plane through the two preauricular points and a point above the nasion, at least {{ F.g2_preauricular_exclusion_mm }} mm from either preauricular point (no face, ear or neck sensors). The **dense array** fills this region by farthest-point sampling with a minimum centre spacing of {{ F.g2_dense_packing_mm }} mm: {{ F.g2_dense_sites }} sites on the adult. The **matched array** puts one OPM at each Neuromag sensor site inside the coverage region, projected onto the scalp: {{ F.g2_matched_sites }} of the {{ F.g2_squid_sites }} sites ({{ F.g2_matched_excluded_sites }} lie outside it). A site whose cell would come within {{ F.g2_cell_clearance_rule_mm }} mm of the head surface was moved outward along its axis ({{ F.g2_dense_moved_out }} dense and {{ F.g2_matched_moved_out }} matched sites on the adult, at most {{ F.g2_dense_max_shift_mm }} mm in the dense array), and the scalp-gap variants of Section 3.4 move the same sites outward. Both arrays are refitted to every head with the same rules (Section 2.5). A triaxial array at the matched sites ({{ F.g2_triax_channels }} channels) controls for channel count. Forward fields were computed with MNE-Python {{ F.g3b_mne_version }}; the arrays are shown in [@fig-noise].

### 2.3 Noise model {#meth-noise}

Every array saw identical noise sources in one analysis band of {{ F.g2_band_range_hz }} Hz (zero-phase Butterworth filter of order {{ F.g2_filter_order }}; equivalent noise bandwidth {{ F.g2_enbw_hz }} Hz):

- *Sensor noise*, white: {{ F.g2_squid_mag_asd }} fT/√Hz for the Neuromag magnetometers and {{ F.g2_squid_grad_asd }} fT/(cm √Hz) for the gradiometers (typical values of the TRIUX system, not verified against its specification sheet), and {{ F.g2_opm_asd_primary }} fT/√Hz for the OPMs in the primary analysis (swept from {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_range }} fT/√Hz). The OPM value is a declared parameter, not a measured device value: it is the middle of the sweep and within the range of sensor noise quoted by the clinical OPM studies (Section 4.2), while the top of the sweep is close to the empty-room floor read from the preprint's recording (Section 3.2); the comparisons are reported across the sweep.
- *Cortical background*: {{ F.g2_n_background }} independent dipoles normal to the cortex on a {{ F.g2_bg_grid_mm }}-mm grid, weighted by cortical area. Like the targets, it omits the cortex within {{ F.g2_source_skull_clearance_mm }} mm of the inner skull ({{ F.g2_excluded_vertices_pct_1dp }} of the vertices), the sources nearest to every sensor. One scale was fitted so that the median good gradiometer's in-band variance equals the measured brain noise of the sample recording: the in-band variance of the pre-stimulus windows of its {{ F.g2_measured_baseline_windows }} stimuli, which begin soon after the previous stimulus and so hold task-related rather than only resting activity, minus that of the empty-room recording (the channel marked bad excluded). Spatially correlated variants used correlation lengths of {{ F.g2_bg_corr_lengths_mm }} mm.
- *Room field*: an external field of {{ F.g2_room_terms }} terms (uniform plus linear gradient) fitted to the empty-room recording and passed through each array's coil integration.

The noise conditions are sensor noise only; sensor plus brain noise (the primary condition); plus the room field; and projected, in which the external subspace of the room field is removed from every array by the same noise-weighted projection. Both configurations named two headline conditions before any run, sensor plus brain noise and projected, and the main results are reported in both. In the band sensitivity analysis ({{ F.g2_band_1_10hz_label }}, {{ F.g2_band_8_30hz_label }} and {{ F.g2_band_30_80hz_label }} Hz) the brain scale and the room field were refitted in each band. The model was checked against the measured Neuromag recording and, in adaptations to this anatomy of two published adult simulation studies, against their depth-orientation maps of spike SNR (Hunold et al., 2016) and their whole-cortex SNR maps (Goldenholz et al., 2009), each with an OPM column added ([S1](benchmarks.html)). Each adaptation keeps its paper's design rather than this study's model: Hunold et al.'s {{ F.g1b_dipole_peak_nam }}-nAm spike-wave sources over a background of randomly placed cortical dipoles, conductivities of {{ F.g1b_conductivity }} S/m and their peak-to-peak SNR, and Goldenholz et al.'s {{ F.g1c_focal_nam }}-nAm dipoles, channel-averaged power SNR and a cortical background calibrated on recorded noise.

### 2.4 Detectability and other metrics {#meth-metric}

The primary metric is known-topography detectability, d = √(sᵀC⁻¹s): the output SNR of the optimal matched filter for a source of known field pattern s in noise of covariance C, computed with the true ('oracle') covariance and rank-aware whitening for every target and system. We report the median over targets of the paired ratio d~OPM~/d~Neuromag~ (not the ratio of the two systems' median detectabilities), or D = 20 log~10~(d~OPM~/d~Neuromag~) in dB, so that a positive D favours the OPM; D does not depend on the dipole moment. The comparator is Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels unless stated. In the adult comparison (Sections 3.3 to 3.5) the medians are unweighted over all {{ F.g2_n_targets }} targets, the {{ F.g2_medialwall_n }} on the medial wall included; in the pediatric comparison (Sections 3.6 to 3.9) D is an area-weighted median without the medial wall. At the adult's measured pose the dense array's ratio is {{ F.g3b_link_g2_ratio_all_targets_3dp }} unweighted over all targets and {{ F.g3b_link_g2_ratio_cortical_3dp }} without the medial wall, and its D is {{ F.g3b_link_g2_db }} dB area-weighted.

Peak-channel SNR is the largest ratio of a channel's noise-free signal to its own in-band noise standard deviation over a system's channels, so that magnetometers and gradiometers compete on equal terms; it is the closest analogue of the clinical studies' sensor-level SNRs. Mean-power SNR is 10 log~10~ of the channel mean of the squared signal-to-noise ratios (the channel-averaged SNR of Goldenholz et al., 2009, also used by Matsubara et al., 2026), compared as an amplitude ratio. Plug-in detectability is the output SNR, under the true covariance, of the matched filter built from a Ledoit–Wolf estimate (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004), so it cannot exceed the oracle value; the estimate uses {{ F.g2_plugin10s_samples }} or {{ F.g2_plugin60s_samples }} independent samples, the time-bandwidth (2BT) equivalent of {{ F.g2_cov_seconds }} s of data in this band, drawn for all arrays from one common noise realization. To bridge to the sphere, the equal-SNR depth was recomputed on the real head with the sphere's own quantities: peak-channel SNR against the Neuromag magnetometers with sensor noise only, and d~eq~(η) from depth-binned medians.

### 2.5 Smaller heads, helmet placements and contrasts {#meth-pediatric}

The smaller heads belong to three classes that are not independent subjects ([@tab-anatomies]). The {{ F.g3b_n_scaled_heads }} **scaled adults** are copies of the adult scaled by one factor (size only): the school-age size control at {{ F.g3b_cfg_school_scale }} times the adult, and the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_years }}-year size control at the head circumference of the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_months }}-month template. The {{ F.g3b_n_templates_heads }} **infant templates** at {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_months }}, {{ F.g3b_infant18mo_age_months }} and {{ F.g3b_infant12mo_age_months }} months (O'Reilly et al., 2021) average many MRIs of the Neurodevelopmental MRI Database (Richards et al., 2016) and have smooth cortices; they were used at their native size with their own three-layer BEMs, oct-6 source spaces, atlas labels and fiducials. The {{ F.g3b_n_children_heads }} **individual school-aged children** A, B and C ({{ F.g3b_children_age_range }} years) are typically developing children of the OpenNeuro dataset ds005234 (Fadeev et al., 2024, 2025), with their own MRIs processed by the dataset's authors. Because the dataset's boundary-element surfaces put the inner skull implausibly close to the scalp (a median {{ F.g3b_children_watershed_inner_skull_depth_p50_range }} mm below it over the upper head), the children's skull was rebuilt: the inner skull lies {{ F.g3b_cfg_child_skull_depth }} mm below the scalp wherever the dataset's lay shallower, with no vertex within {{ F.g3b_cfg_child_skull_min_cortex }} mm of the white surface, and the outer skull about halfway between it and the scalp. Every head used the adult's conductivities; with a single-compartment BEM, which needs no skull or scalp conductivity, the difference of medians from the adult is {{ F.g3b_smaller_bem1_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_range }} dB, against {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_range }} dB with three layers ([S3](pediatric.html)). The children's fiducials, which define the head frame and so the centred placement, the origin of the counterfactual scaling and the OPM coverage region, were transferred from the adult by a similarity fit of the cortices, which on the templates reproduces their own fiducials to within {{ F.g3b_templates_fid_transfer_range }} mm and their head frames to within {{ F.g3b_templates_fid_transfer_rotation_range }}°.

**Fixed adult helmet.** The same Neuromag sensors and noise were used for every head. The *centred* placement puts each head's own head frame where the adult's lies in the sample recording (the recording's device-to-head transform), so that the ear line stays where the adult's was. The primary placement is *top contact*: the head is raised from the centred pose until the nearest magnetometer coil centre is {{ F.g3b_cfg_clearance }} mm from the scalp (the {{ F.g3b_cfg_dewar_spacing }}-mm Dewar spacing plus a margin). This is a best-fit assumption rather than documented practice: a child in an adult helmet may be placed centrally and repositioned towards the expected source (Gaetz et al., 2008), and a small child's shoulders can stop the head short of the top (Tesan et al., 2010), so top contact favours Neuromag; the other placements bound this choice. A family of {{ F.g3b_n_family_placements }} placements chosen from the geometry alone tests placement sensitivity ({{ F.g3b_child_c_n_family_feasible }} for child C, for whom one lateral shift was infeasible): top contact, back contact, shifts of ±{{ F.g3b_cfg_translation }} mm left–right and front–back, and rotations of ±{{ F.g3b_cfg_pitch }}° pitch, ±{{ F.g3b_cfg_roll }}° roll and ±{{ F.g3b_cfg_yaw }}° yaw, each raised to the same contact. Further placements are the centred pose itself, a laterally centred pose raised to contact, and true contact at the Dewar spacing.

**Counterfactual helmet.** Every coil centre is scaled about the head origin by the child-to-adult ratio of head circumferences, keeping coil size, orientation and noise, with the factor raised until no coil is within {{ F.g3b_cfg_dewar_spacing }} mm of the scalp. The helmet is thus scaled in proportion to the head, gaps included, unlike the sphere's constant gap. It was computed about the centred head and, as a variant added after the first pediatric results to remove the templates' lateral offset, about the laterally centred head; the laterally centred version is the one reported as primary, a choice made after the analyses. It is a geometric construction that asks what the adult's fit would give at a child's size, not a model of a pediatric SQUID device.

**OPM arrays and noise.** The OPM arrays were refitted to each head with the adult's rules: {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_sites_range }} dense and {{ F.g3b_smaller_matched_sites_range }} matched sites on the smaller heads, at a median standoff of {{ F.g3b_all_dense_standoff_2dp_range }} mm on every head. Sensor noise, room field and band were unchanged, and the cortical background variance per unit cortical area was calibrated once on the adult, so a smaller cortex carries less total background (multiplied by {{ F.g3b_cfg_bg_factor_low }} and by {{ F.g3b_cfg_bg_factor_high }} as a sensitivity analysis).

**Estimands.** For each head, D is the area-weighted median over cortical targets (medial wall excluded). The change from the adult, Δ = D~child~ − D~adult~, is vertex-wise for the scaled adults and, for the templates and children, which have no vertex correspondence, the adult-area-weighted median of parcel-level differences. In every placement and helmet, D~adult~ is the adult's D under the same rule: at its own top-contact, centred, laterally centred or true-contact pose, and in its own counterfactual helmets, whose scale factor is {{ F.g3b_adult_cfx_k }}. Within strata of depth below each head's own scalp (edges at {{ F.g3b_cfg_depth_edges }} mm), Δ is instead the difference between the two heads' area-weighted medians in the stratum, for every head including the scaled adults, with intervals from resampling parcels in each head independently; strata with fewer than {{ F.g3b_cfg_min_n }} targets in either head are not summarized. A depth-reweighted comparison weights each head's targets to the adult's depth distribution (a difference of pooled medians), and a channel-count control subsamples the adult's dense array to each smaller head's site count. Where any other difference of two pooled medians is used, we say so. Δ is reported descriptively, without a test or a margin.

### 2.6 Brain regions {#meth-regions}

Regions are the lobes of the Desikan–Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006) as grouped by the study (frontal: superior, rostral middle and caudal middle frontal, pars opercularis, triangularis and orbitalis, lateral and medial orbitofrontal, precentral, paracentral and frontal pole; parietal: superior and inferior parietal, supramarginal, postcentral and precuneus; temporal: superior, middle and inferior temporal, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, fusiform, transverse temporal, entorhinal, temporal pole and parahippocampal; occipital: lateral occipital, lingual, cuneus and pericalcarine; cingulate: rostral and caudal anterior, posterior and isthmus; insula; the medial wall belongs to none), and single parcels that the epilepsy literature emphasizes: precentral, superior temporal ('lateral temporal') and parahippocampal, with a mesial temporal group of the parahippocampal and entorhinal parcels; 'mesial temporal' always means this group. Both hemispheres are pooled. All sources lie on the cortical surface; hippocampus and amygdala are not modelled, so the parahippocampal and entorhinal cortex, {{ F.reg_mesial_parcels_depth_range }} mm below the scalp in the adult by the depth measure of Section 2.2 (median over targets), are the nearest proxies for mesial temporal generators. The regions were chosen from the literature, and rankings among them are exploratory.

### 2.7 Simulated interictal spikes {#meth-spikes}

The detection study runs the noise model in the time domain: background moments with a 1/f spectrum, a room field with the cross-spectra of the empty-room recording and white sensor noise, each scaled so that its in-band covariance equals that of Section 2.3 (the room field is not projected out), filtered to {{ F.g4_band_lo_hz }}–{{ F.g4_band_hi_hz }} Hz and decimated to {{ F.g4_sampling_rate_hz }} Hz, with one realization per {{ F.g4_segment_s }}-s segment shared by all arrays. The spike is the spike-wave complex of Hunold et al. (2016), digitised, stretched in time by {{ F.g4_stretches }} ({{ F.g4_n_morphologies }} morphologies) and passed through the same filter. Spikes were placed at {{ F.g4_n_locations }} locations stratified by depth below the scalp ({{ F.g4_band_10_20mm }}, {{ F.g4_band_20_30mm }}, {{ F.g4_band_30_45mm }} and {{ F.g4_band_45_70mm }} mm; {{ F.g4_locations_per_band }} locations per band) and by orientation ({{ F.g4_n_orientation_bands }} bands from radial to tangential; {{ F.g4_locations_per_stratum }} locations per depth and orientation stratum). Each location received {{ F.g4_n_focal_events_per_location }} focal-dipole events ({{ F.g4_n_strengths }} strengths of {{ F.g4_strengths_nam }} nAm, each with every morphology) and patch events of {{ F.g4_patch_radius_mm }} mm radius carrying the same total moments with the unstretched morphology: {{ F.g4_n_focal_events }} focal and {{ F.g4_n_patch_events }} patch events, {{ F.g4_n_events_per_anatomy }} per anatomy, injected in random order one every {{ F.g4_event_spacing_s }} s, identically in every array.

Every array was read by two detectors, and Neuromag also by its magnetometers and its gradiometers alone ({{ F.g4_n_detectors }} detectors per anatomy). Both detectors whiten the data with a Ledoit–Wolf covariance from {{ F.g4_null_baseline_minutes }} min of null data and filter them with the spike templates. The oracle knows source and time: it tests the whitened, template-filtered projection on the true field pattern at the true peak (one-sided) against a threshold for a per-trial false-positive probability of {{ F.g4_oracle_alpha }}. The practical detector knows neither: at every sample it takes the largest normalized output over the {{ F.g4_n_morphologies }} templates and {{ F.g4_n_dictionary_range }} candidate field patterns on a {{ F.g4_dictionary_spacing_mm }}-mm cortical grid that excludes the true sources; its local maxima at least {{ F.g4_refractory_s }} s apart are events, and a spike counts as detected when an event above threshold lies within ±{{ F.g4_hit_tolerance_ms }} ms of its peak, the same events being counted as false on null data. The templates are the simulated morphologies and the candidates' field patterns come from the true forward model, which makes the practical detector optimistic for every array; whether equally so was not tested, and focal on-scalp patterns may be more sensitive to forward-model and coregistration error. Its thresholds for {{ F.g4_operating_point_per_minute }} false event per minute were set on {{ F.g4_null_calibration_minutes }} min of independent null data and frozen; {{ F.g4_null_heldout_minutes }} min of held-out null data gave {{ F.g4_heldout_rate_range }} false events per minute across detectors and anatomies.

S50 is the strength at which the detection rate of a band's focal events ({{ F.g4_n_focal_events_per_band }} events: the morphologies pooled over the band's {{ F.g4_locations_per_band }} locations) reaches {{ F.g4_s50_level }}, interpolated linearly in log strength between the tested strengths; the patch events enter neither S50 nor the paired tests. The paired ratio S50~Neuromag~/S50~OPM~ is above 1 when the OPM needs less strength. The adult sits at its measured pose and the smaller heads at top contact; the measured pose gives the adult a D {{ F.g3b_adult_centred_minus_top_d_abs }} dB higher than top contact.

Localization was examined on {{ F.loc_n_locations }} locations with focal and patch sources of {{ F.loc_strengths_nam }} nAm, one event per source type and strength at each location ({{ F.loc_events_per_condition }} per condition, unstretched morphology) in independent null data. The inverse used a single-compartment BEM (the simulation used three layers), one of {{ F.loc_coreg_draws }} coregistration-error draws of {{ F.loc_coreg_shift_mm }} mm and {{ F.loc_coreg_angle_deg }}° per location, the same for every array, a Ledoit–Wolf noise covariance from {{ F.loc_baseline_minutes }} min of null data and a fixed-orientation source grid at {{ F.loc_grid_spacing_mm }}-mm spacing ({{ F.loc_inverse_grid_range }} sources) that excludes the true source vertices. Dipoles were fitted with MNE-Python at least {{ F.loc_ecd_min_dist_mm }} mm inside the inner skull, and dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM; Dale et al., 2000), with depth weighting {{ F.loc_depth_weighting }} and a regularization set by an assumed SNR of {{ F.loc_snr }}, was computed in the study's implementation and in MNE-Python's, at the true peak sample. The error is the distance from the true source (a patch's centre) to the dipole or to the dSPM peak, read on the MRI through the perturbed transform; joint success is detection by the practical detector (thresholds from {{ F.loc_calibration_minutes }} min of null data) with an error of at most {{ F.loc_joint_threshold_mm }} mm. Paired error differences were tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and joint success with an exact McNemar test.

Head motion and slippage of the OPM cap were bounded rather than simulated in time, on the adult and the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_months }}- and {{ F.g3b_infant12mo_age_months }}-month templates at top contact: the head was displaced in the fixed helmet by up to {{ F.mot_translation_largest_mm }} mm or rotated about the head origin, and the dense OPM cap rotated on the head by up to {{ F.mot_slip_largest_deg }}°, sensors that would enter the scalp being lifted; each case was evaluated with the matched filter of the reference geometry (uncompensated) and with the displaced geometry known ([S4](epilepsy.html)).

### 2.8 Statistics {#meth-stats}

Neighbouring cortical targets are correlated, so the adult's intervals are {{ F.g2_ci_level_pct }} bootstrap intervals over {{ F.g2_n_parcels }} labels (the atlas parcels and the two medial-wall labels), and the pediatric intervals resample parcels within each anatomy. All intervals are percentile intervals. They describe spatial variation within one anatomy, not variability between subjects, and exclude placement and construction uncertainty. The adult's primary, lobe and triaxial intervals and the dense array's comparisons at top contact and their depth strata use {{ F.g3b_cfg_n_boot }} resamples; the adult's sensitivity analyses (the OPM sensor-noise sweep, scalp gaps, head shifts and the background, band and extended-source variants), the matched-array comparisons of the smaller heads, every other placement (both counterfactual helmets included) and the channel-count control use fewer, so their interval ends are less precise. In the spike study the location is the statistical unit: S50 ratios have location-bootstrap intervals ({{ F.g4_bootstrap_resamples }} resamples), open at one end when resamples do not reach half detection within the tested strengths, and paired detection differences are tested with an exact sign-flip test on per-location differences in detection counts.

No endpoint, test or margin was declared before the analyses, although both configurations named the two headline noise conditions in advance (Section 2.3). We name the endpoints here as chosen after the analyses: (i) the dense array's detectability ratio against Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels in the adult; (ii) Δ of the dense array against the same comparator at top contact in the fixed helmet and in the counterfactual helmet scaled about the laterally centred head, each against the adult placed by the same rule, reported descriptively (for the counterfactual helmet with the room field projected out only the pooled medians are stored, so there it is a difference of medians); and (iii) for the spikes, the dense array against Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels in the {{ F.g4_band_10_20mm }} mm band with the practical detector, tested with the two-sided exact sign-flip test on per-location differences in detection counts, Holm-corrected (Holm, 1979) over the {{ F.g4_n_anatomies }} anatomies, with the paired S50 ratio and its location-bootstrap interval as the effect size. This band, array and detector were chosen from the {{ F.g4_family_per_opm_array }} comparisons per OPM array of each anatomy, a selection that the correction over anatomies does not control; Section 3.10 also reports a Bonferroni correction over those {{ F.g4_family_per_opm_array }}. All other comparisons are exploratory and reported with uncorrected p-values. The detection family has {{ F.g4_family_per_anatomy }} comparisons per anatomy (two OPM arrays against Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels, its gradiometers and its magnetometers, with both detectors, in {{ F.g4_n_depth_bands }} depth bands) and the localization family {{ F.loc_family_per_anatomy }} (two OPM arrays; focal and patch sources at two strengths; the dipole error, the two dSPM errors, and joint detection and localization with the study's dSPM or with the dipole).

The model went through several versions. The last change, equal OPM standoff on every head, was made after the pediatric results were known. It lowered Δ by {{ F.g3b_v3_v4_delta_drop_range }} dB in the scaled adults and templates (the school-aged children were added afterwards and exist only in the final version), raised the adult's projected dense ratio (an earlier construction gave {{ F.g2_hse_v3_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }}; Section 3.4), made the laterally centred counterfactual Δ of the scaled adults and templates negative, and strengthened the adult spike result (earlier {{ F.g4_earlier_v3_dense_vs_combined_10_20mm }} locations, p = {{ F.g4_earlier_v3_dense_vs_combined_10_20mm_p }}). The laterally centred placement and its counterfactual helmet were also added after the first pediatric results. Results that depend on these choices are post hoc, the earlier result files are not part of the stored results, and persistence across versions is not replication.

### 3.2 An explicit noise model: what it reproduces and what it assumes {#res-noise}

*What sets η in a real recording, and how far can the noise model be trusted?* Four kinds of agreement must be kept apart ([@fig-noise]b). For the magnetometers the room field was *fitted* to the empty-room recording: with the manufacturer's sensor noise it reproduces their empty-room level ({{ F.g2_model_empty_room_mag_ft }} against {{ F.g2_measured_empty_room_mag_ft }} fT measured) and explains {{ F.g2_room_explained_mag_pct }} of their empty-room variance. For the gradiometers the room field explains only {{ F.g2_room_explained_grad_pct }} of that variance, so their empty-room agreement ({{ F.g2_model_empty_room_grad_ftcm }} against {{ F.g2_measured_empty_room_grad_ftcm }} fT/cm measured) is an independent *check* of the manufacturer's sensor noise, which slightly exceeds the measured level. The gradiometer brain noise is *calibrated*: {{ F.g2_model_brain_grad_ftcm }} fT/cm, equal to the measurement by construction. The magnetometer brain noise is the one independent *prediction* of the background, and it falls short: {{ F.g2_model_brain_mag_ft }} fT modelled against {{ F.g2_measured_brain_mag_ft }} fT measured ({{ F.g2_brain_mag_model_over_measured_ratio }} times). The measured value is the excess of the task baseline over the empty room, which also holds non-cortical physiological fields (cardiac, ocular) that the model omits by design, so the shortfall is not purely an error of the cortical background. Nothing about the OPM noise is measured: its sensor noise is declared, and its brain and room noise are the Neuromag-calibrated model seen through the OPM arrays' own lead fields.

The two published simulation studies check shape and level. The depth-orientation maps of Hunold et al. (2016), adapted to this anatomy, correlate with the published dipole maps at r = {{ F.g1b_dipole_r_range }} but reach only {{ F.g1b_dipole_level_range }} times their level (patch maps: r = {{ F.g1b_patch_r_range }}, {{ F.g1b_patch_level_range }} times), even with a background scale fitted to the paper's example traces. The calibration rule of Goldenholz et al. (2009) gives a noise-source amplitude within their published range once normalized to their number of sources ({{ F.g1c_source_sd_4000_nam }} against {{ F.g1c_paper_source_sd_nam_range }} nAm; {{ F.g1c_source_sd_nam }} nAm on our grid). Both adaptations already show the dependence on metric that Section 3.4 examines. With brain noise only, a matched OPM array's best single channel in our adaptation of Hunold's dipole maps reaches only {{ F.g1b_opm_vs_grad_rows_range }} times the SNR of the planar gradiometers in every depth row, and {{ F.g1b_opm_vs_mag_rows_range }} times that of the magnetometers, falling with depth, whereas with Goldenholz's channel-averaged SNR the OPM ties both ({{ F.g1c_opm_minus_grad_focal_brain_only_db }} dB against the gradiometers and {{ F.g1c_opm_minus_mag_focal_brain_only_db }} dB against the magnetometers; [S1](benchmarks.html)).

In this model brain noise dominates both kinds of magnetometer channel: it is {{ F.g2_noise_share_dense_brain_pct }} of a dense-array OPM channel's in-band variance (room field {{ F.g2_noise_share_dense_room_pct }}, sensor noise {{ F.g2_noise_share_dense_sensor_pct }}) and {{ F.g2_noise_share_squid_mag_brain_pct }} of a Neuromag magnetometer's (room field {{ F.g2_noise_share_squid_mag_room_pct }}, sensor noise {{ F.g2_noise_share_squid_mag_sensor_pct }}), whereas sensor noise is {{ F.g2_noise_share_squid_grad_sensor_pct }} of a gradiometer's ([@fig-noise]a, c). In RMS terms a dense-array channel carries {{ F.g2_noise_dense_brain }} fT of brain noise, {{ F.g2_noise_dense_room }} fT of room field and {{ F.g2_noise_dense_intrinsic }} fT of sensor noise in the band, against {{ F.g2_noise_squid_mag_brain }}, {{ F.g2_noise_squid_mag_room }} and {{ F.g2_noise_squid_mag_intrinsic }} fT at the magnetometers. The implied single-channel noise ratio OPM/magnetometer, the analogue of η, is about {{ F.g2_noise_rms_dense_vs_mag_noroom_ratio_1dp }} in the {{ F.g2_band_range_hz }} Hz band with sensor and brain noise, the primary condition ({{ F.g2_noise_rms_dense_vs_mag_ratio_1dp }} with the room field added), against about {{ F.lit_sef_noise_ratio_closest }} in the preprint's recording over a much wider band ({{ F.lit_sef_band_range_hz }} Hz). Approximate readings of the preprint's spectra, not stored results, locate the difference: where brain activity dominates, the OPM/SQUID ratio of the spectral peaks is about {{ F.lit_sef_midband_noise_ratio }}, somewhat below the model's {{ F.g2_noise_rms_dense_vs_mag_noroom_ratio_1dp }}, whereas the measured OPM has an empty-room floor of about {{ F.lit_sef_opm_floor_asd_range }} fT/√Hz and a distance-independent low-frequency component of about {{ F.lit_sef_opm_lowfreq_peak_asd_range }} fT/√Hz at its peak. On this reading the model lacks the OPM's own floor and a non-brain low-frequency component, which would explain why the measured OPM noise barely fell when the sensors were lifted.

The primary results therefore assume a white OPM noise of {{ F.g2_opm_asd_primary }} fT/√Hz and no non-brain physiological noise, with a modelled brain noise that under-predicts the measured one. Section 3.4 shows what happens when the OPM floor rises.

### 3.3 The realistic adult: a modest whole-array advantage, largest near the scalp {#res-adult}

*With explicit noise, which system detects a cortical source better, and where?* With sensor and brain noise, the dense array's median paired detectability ratio over the {{ F.g2_n_targets }} targets is {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci_3dp }}, and the OPM is ahead for all but {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_n_squid_better }} targets. The matched array's ratio is {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci_3dp }}, ahead for {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_share }} of the targets: its interval includes 1, but no equivalence margin was set, and its standing changes with the condition (below and Section 3.4). With sensor noise only, Neuromag leads both ({{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_int_ratio_3dp }} and {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_int_ratio_3dp }}), and the dense array would need a sensor noise below {{ F.g2_breakeven_dense_vs_combined_asd }} fT/√Hz to break even.

The advantage is largest near the scalp ([@fig-adult]a). The dense array's ratio is {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_ib_10_15_ratio }} in the {{ F.g2_depth_bin_10_15_mm }} mm bin, which holds {{ F.g2_depth_10_15_n }} of the targets, {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_ib_25_30_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_25_30_mm }} mm, and {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_ib_35_60_range }} in the bins from {{ F.g2_depth_bin_35_40_mm }} to {{ F.g2_depth_bin_55_60_mm }} mm; the matched array's falls from {{ F.g2_depth_matched_vs_combined_ib_10_15_ratio }} to below 1 in every bin from {{ F.g2_depth_matched_vs_combined_ib_below1_from_bin }} mm down. The projection that removes the room field costs Neuromag {{ F.g2_proj_cost_squid_pct_2sf }} of its detectability, the dense array {{ F.g2_proj_cost_dense_pct }} and the matched array {{ F.g2_proj_cost_matched_pct }}, relative to the room-field condition without projection; after it the ratios are {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_proj_ci_3dp }} and {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_proj_ci_3dp }}, so that the matched array is then behind Neuromag. At depth both projected ratios reach or fall below 1: the dense array's is {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_proj_45_50_ratio_3dp }} and {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_proj_50_55_ratio_3dp }} in the {{ F.g2_depth_bin_45_50_mm }} and {{ F.g2_depth_bin_50_55_mm }} mm bins and falls clearly below 1 only from {{ F.g2_depth_bin_55_60_mm }} mm ({{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_proj_55_60_ratio_3dp }}; {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_proj_60_65_ratio_3dp }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_60_65_mm }} mm), and the matched array's falls below 1 from the {{ F.g2_depth_matched_vs_combined_proj_below1_from_bin }} mm bin ({{ F.g2_depth_matched_vs_combined_proj_25_30_ratio_3dp }}). No per-bin intervals are stored, so these crossings are descriptive. A crossing therefore exists in this model as well, but it is not the sphere's equal-SNR depth: it belongs to whole-array detectability after a projection, against {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels, and it moves with the OPM noise.

Why does the ratio fall with depth? Against Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels the fall is already present with sensor noise alone, from {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_int_10_15_ratio }} to {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_combined_int_bins_min }}, because Neuromag's sensor-noise-only detectability rests on its magnetometers, and the peak-field ratio OPM/magnetometer falls from {{ F.g2_leadfield_dense_vs_mag_10_15_ratio }} in the shallowest bin to {{ F.g2_leadfield_dense_vs_mag_bins_min }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_55_60_mm }} mm ([@fig-adult]b); the detectability ratio falls with it, though less steeply, since the whole-array measure draws on the field at many channels. Against the {{ F.g2_squid_grad_channels }} gradiometers alone the sensor-noise-only ratio barely changes with depth ({{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_grad_int_10_15_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_10_15_mm }} mm, {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_grad_int_20_25_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_20_25_mm }} mm and {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_grad_int_60_65_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_60_65_mm }} mm), yet with brain noise it falls from {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_grad_ib_10_15_ratio }} to {{ F.g2_depth_dense_vs_grad_ib_35_40_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_depth_bin_35_40_mm }} mm: there the depth dependence comes from brain noise, which costs every array most for deep sources. Against all {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels, adding brain noise multiplies the dense array's ratio by a nearly constant factor in every depth bin ({{ F.g2_brainmult_dense_vs_combined_range }}), whereas for the matched array the factor rises with depth ({{ F.g2_brainmult_matched_vs_combined_range }}) and partly offsets the geometric fall. The size of the factor follows the per-channel noise budget rather than better whitening by the denser array: brain noise multiplies the in-band noise of a Neuromag magnetometer about {{ F.g2_noise_inflation_squid_mag_1dp }}-fold over its sensor floor but that of an OPM only about {{ F.g2_noise_inflation_dense_1dp }}-fold (a ratio of {{ F.g2_noise_inflation_mag_over_dense_ratio }}), and the sparser matched array has the larger factor. On the cortex ([@fig-adult]c) the dense array is ahead almost everywhere, most on the crowns of the lateral convexity, and the matched array behind mainly on ventral and polar surfaces. How far this ranking depends on choices in which the clinical studies differ is the next question.

### 3.4 What decides the comparison: comparator, metric and noise floor {#res-conditions}

*Which choices change the adult ranking?* [@fig-conditions] varies one factor at a time from the primary condition. The **comparator** matters: against Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_grad_channels }} gradiometers alone the dense array's ratio is {{ F.g2_dense_vs_grad_ib_ratio }} and the matched array's {{ F.g2_matched_vs_grad_ib_ratio }}; against its {{ F.g2_squid_sites }} magnetometers alone, {{ F.g2_dense_vs_mag_ib_ratio }} and {{ F.g2_matched_vs_mag_ib_ratio }}. The **metric** matters more. With peak-channel SNR, the closest analogue of the clinical studies' SNRs, the dense array is ahead of the magnetometers ({{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_mag_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_mag_ib_ci }}; matched {{ F.g2_peak_matched_vs_mag_ib_ratio }}) but behind the best of all {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels, usually a gradiometer ({{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci }}, OPM higher for {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_combined_ib_share }} of targets; matched {{ F.g2_peak_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), and behind the best gradiometer ({{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_grad_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_grad_ib_ci }}; matched {{ F.g2_peak_matched_vs_grad_ib_ratio }}). With mean-power SNR, the channel-averaged metric of Goldenholz et al. (2009) and Matsubara et al. (2026), the dense array is barely ahead of Neuromag ({{ F.g2_meanpow_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_meanpow_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci }}; {{ F.g2_meanpow_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio }} projected), no better than the matched array ({{ F.g2_meanpow_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), and behind the magnetometers ({{ F.g2_meanpow_dense_vs_mag_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_meanpow_dense_vs_mag_ib_ci }}). A covariance estimated from {{ F.g2_plugin10s_samples }} samples costs Neuromag more than the OPMs ({{ F.g2_plugin10s_loss_squid_combined_ib_pct }} against {{ F.g2_plugin10s_loss_dense_ib_pct }} of the oracle detectability), raising the dense ratio to {{ F.g2_plugin10s_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} and the matched ratio to {{ F.g2_plugin10s_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}.

The **noise floor** decides whether there is an advantage at all. As the OPM sensor noise rises from {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_min }} to {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz, the dense array's ratio falls from {{ F.g2_sens_asd7ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} to {{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} ({{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }} projected) and the matched array's from {{ F.g2_sens_asd7ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} to {{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }}; the matched array's interval lies above 1 at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_10 }} fT/√Hz ({{ F.g2_sens_asd10ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_sens_asd10ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci_3dp }}) and below 1 at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_20 }} fT/√Hz ({{ F.g2_sens_asd20ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_sens_asd20ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci_3dp }}). A gap between scalp and sensors does the same: {{ F.g2_sens_gap3mm_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} with a {{ F.g2_opm_gap_small_mm }}-mm gap and {{ F.g2_sens_gap6mm_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} with a {{ F.g2_opm_gap_large_mm }}-mm gap. Together, a {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }}-fT/√Hz floor and a {{ F.g2_opm_gap_small_mm }}-mm gap reverse the ranking ({{ F.g2_joint_gap3mm_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} {{ F.g2_joint_gap3mm_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci_3dp }}).

**Channel count** recovers much of the dense array's lead: at the {{ F.g2_matched_sites }} matched sites, three axes with the same noise on every axis ({{ F.g2_triax_channels }} channels) raise the ratio from {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} to {{ F.g2_triax_equal_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} ({{ F.g2_triax_equal_vs_combined_proj_ratio }} projected), close to the dense array's {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} from {{ F.g2_dense_sites }} single-axis sites, and to {{ F.g2_triax_tang2x_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} if the tangential axes are twice as noisy; the dense array's lead is thus partly a matter of channel count and partly of coverage. No array of only a few sensors was simulated: the smallest are the matched array and, on the smaller heads, dense arrays of {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_sites_range }} sites. Other choices move the dense ratio little: head shifts of ±{{ F.g2_head_translation_mm }} mm along each axis and ±{{ F.g2_head_pitch_deg }}° of pitch ({{ F.g2_sens_headpos_dense_vs_combined_ib_range }}), spatially correlated background ({{ F.g2_sens_bgcorr5mm_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} and {{ F.g2_sens_bgcorr10mm_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), Neuromag sensor noise from its measured empty-room spectrum ({{ F.g2_sens_squidmeas_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), a single-compartment BEM ({{ F.g2_conv_bem1_5120_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}, on a subset of targets), extended sources ({{ F.g2_patches_dense_vs_combined_ib_range }}) and other frequency bands ({{ F.g2_band_1_10hz_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_band_1_10hz_label }} Hz, {{ F.g2_band_30_80hz_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_band_30_80hz_label }} Hz). One construction choice matters more: with nearly radial OPM axes at the current sites, the projected dense ratio is {{ F.g2_hse_v4sites_v3axes_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }} instead of {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }}, and the earlier model version, with different sites as well as those axes, gave {{ F.g2_hse_v3_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio_3dp }}.

The adult result is thus an advantage for a dense, low-noise array; for an array at Neuromag's sites, a ratio whose interval includes 1 with sensor and brain noise and that falls below 1 after projection; and a disadvantage for noisier sensors or for peak-channel SNR against the full system: the respects in which clinical studies differ.

### 3.5 Brain regions in the adult: the dense lead is smallest in cingulate and insular cortex, and the matched array trails on ventral and polar surfaces {#res-regions}

*Which regions favour the OPM, and under which comparator?* With sensor and brain noise and against Neuromag's {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels the dense array is ahead in every lobe ([@fig-regions-adult]a), most in the frontal lobe ({{ F.g2_lobe_frontal_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_frontal_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci }}) and least in the cingulate ({{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci }}) and the insula ({{ F.g2_lobe_insula_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}, an interval resting on only {{ F.g2_lobe_insula_n_parcels }} parcels), with the occipital lobe at {{ F.g2_lobe_occipital_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_occipital_dense_vs_combined_ib_ci }} and the temporal lobe at {{ F.g2_lobe_temporal_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}; after projection the cingulate falls to {{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_dense_vs_combined_proj_ci }}. Of the three single parcels, the precentral gyrus favours it most ({{ F.reg_precentral_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), the superior temporal gyrus is intermediate ({{ F.reg_superiortemporal_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}) and the parahippocampal gyrus lowest ({{ F.reg_parahippocampal_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}; the mesial temporal group {{ F.reg_mesial_temporal_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}, {{ F.reg_mesial_temporal_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio }} projected). That ranking holds only among the regions chosen from the epilepsy literature: of all {{ F.reg_dk_n_parcels }} atlas parcels, {{ F.reg_dk_dense_vs_combined_ib_below_parahippocampal_count }} have a smaller dense lead than the parahippocampal gyrus, the smallest in the posterior cingulate ({{ F.reg_dk_posteriorcingulate_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), the precuneus ({{ F.reg_dk_precuneus_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}) and the caudal anterior cingulate ({{ F.reg_dk_caudalanteriorcingulate_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}). The matched array is ahead in the parietal lobe ({{ F.g2_lobe_parietal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_parietal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci }}), level frontally ({{ F.g2_lobe_frontal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_frontal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci }}) and temporally ({{ F.g2_lobe_temporal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_lobe_temporal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ci }}), and behind in the insula ({{ F.g2_lobe_insula_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}) and in the mesial temporal group ({{ F.reg_mesial_temporal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}; {{ F.reg_mesial_temporal_matched_vs_combined_proj_ratio }} projected), its lowest of the ten regions. Over all parcels it trails more in {{ F.reg_dk_matched_vs_combined_ib_below_mesial_temporal_count }} parcels outside the group, mostly on ventral and polar surfaces: the frontal pole ({{ F.reg_dk_frontalpole_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex ({{ F.reg_dk_medialorbitofrontal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} and {{ F.reg_dk_lateralorbitofrontal_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), pars orbitalis ({{ F.reg_dk_parsorbitalis_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), the temporal pole ({{ F.reg_dk_temporalpole_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}) and the rostral anterior cingulate ({{ F.reg_dk_rostralanteriorcingulate_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}). Against Neuromag's magnetometers alone ([@fig-regions-adult]b) the matched array is below 1 in the parahippocampal gyrus ({{ F.reg_parahippocampal_matched_vs_mag_ib_ratio }}) and the insula ({{ F.g2_lobe_insula_matched_vs_mag_ib_ratio }}) and level in the cingulate ({{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_matched_vs_mag_ib_ratio }}), whereas the dense array stays ahead (parahippocampal {{ F.reg_parahippocampal_dense_vs_mag_ib_ratio }}). These rankings compare point estimates and are exploratory.

Depth to the nearest scalp point understates how far ventromedial cortex is from both arrays. The parahippocampal targets lie at a median {{ F.reg_parahippocampal_depth_mm }} mm, about as deep as the superior temporal ones ({{ F.reg_superiortemporal_depth_mm }} mm) and much shallower than the cingulate and insula targets ({{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_depth_mm }} and {{ F.g2_lobe_insula_depth_mm }} mm; [@fig-regions-adult]c), yet their {{ F.g2_focal_nam }}-nAm fields at the best magnetometer and the best dense-array OPM ({{ F.reg_parahippocampal_amp_mag_ft }} and {{ F.reg_parahippocampal_amp_dense_ft }} fT) match those of the cingulate lobe ({{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_amp_mag_ft }} and {{ F.g2_lobe_cingulate_amp_dense_ft }} fT) and are much weaker than the superior temporal ones ({{ F.reg_superiortemporal_amp_mag_ft }} and {{ F.reg_superiortemporal_amp_dense_ft }} fT). Their low peak-field ratio ({{ F.reg_parahippocampal_peakfield_dense_vs_mag_ratio }}, against {{ F.reg_superiortemporal_peakfield_dense_vs_mag_ratio }} for superior temporal and {{ F.reg_precentral_peakfield_dense_vs_mag_ratio }} for precentral targets; [@fig-regions-adult]d) is what more distant sources give. These peak-field ratios lie within the range of the IED amplitude ratios that Feys et al. (2022) measured against 102 SQUID magnetometers in five school-aged children with extratemporal foci (2.3 to 4.6), while the detectability ratios are much smaller: a larger signal does not give a proportionally larger SNR. Relative to Neuromag, then, the model reproduces for the site-matched array the clinical observation that mesial temporal sources are among the hardest for OPMs, and shows that the answer depends on the array: a dense array keeps a lead there, an array at the SQUID sites does not. The following sections ask how these adult results change in smaller heads: over the whole cortex (Sections 3.6 and 3.7), by region (Section 3.8) and with a noisier OPM (Section 3.9).

### 3.6 Smaller heads in the fixed adult helmet: D rises in every one at top contact {#res-children}

*Does the OPM's relative performance change when the head is smaller and the SQUID helmet stays the adult's?* At top contact the smaller heads are raised {{ F.g3b_smaller_top_moved_range }} mm from the centred pose (the adult's measured pose), and the median magnetometer-to-scalp gap is {{ F.g3b_smaller_top_gap_range }} mm against {{ F.g3b_adult_top_gap }} mm for the adult ([@tab-anatomies]; [@fig-children], top). ### 3.7 Head size or helmet fit {#res-fit}

*Is the smaller heads' extra gain a matter of head size, as the sphere suggests, or of the helmet?* In the sphere with the preprint's size-following shell ([@fig-fit], panels A to D), the equal-SNR depth at η = {{ F.lit_eta_reference }} hardly moves from adult to newborn ({{ F.g3a_follow_eta3_deq_range }} mm), but the normalized depth rises from {{ F.g3a_adult_follow_eta3_norm_1dp }} to {{ F.g3a_newborn_follow_eta3_norm_1dp }} because the brain and the extracerebral layer ({{ F.lit_table1_extracerebral_range_mm }} mm in the preprint's heads) shrink. With one adult shell for every head, size and fit are confounded: the OPM is then ahead at every depth of the newborn and {{ F.g3a_infant1yr_age_years }}-year brains up to η = {{ F.g3a_newborn_fixed_eta0_1dp }} and {{ F.g3a_infant1yr_fixed_eta0_1dp }}. The sphere's prediction is thus conditional on a shell that follows the head at a constant gap.

In the realistic heads, fit and head size act on D through four routes: a wider SQUID gap and a shallower cortex raise it, while fewer OPM sites and, in the school-aged children, a near-adult cortex close to the OPMs lower it. We first change the fit, then match site count and depth. Every Δ is taken against the adult placed by the same rule, because the adult's own D depends on it ([@fig-fit]a): {{ F.g3b_adult_centred_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB at its measured pose, {{ F.g3b_adult_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB at top contact, {{ F.g3b_adult_xcentred_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB laterally centred, {{ F.g3b_adult_top18mm_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB at true contact, and {{ F.g3b_adult_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} and {{ F.g3b_adult_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB in its own counterfactual helmets (centred and laterally centred). In the fixed helmet the smaller heads gain {{ F.g3b_smaller_centred_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB at the centred pose, {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB at top contact, {{ F.g3b_smaller_xcentred_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB laterally centred and {{ F.g3b_smaller_top18mm_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB at true contact. In the counterfactual helmet scaled with the head about the laterally centred head, Δ is {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB. Every upper interval bound is at or below {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_ci_hi_max }} dB (parcel-bootstrap intervals, which exclude placement and construction uncertainty), and only child C, whose scaled helmet keeps a median gap of {{ F.g3b_child_c_cfx_gap }} mm against the adult's {{ F.g3b_adult_cfx_gap }} mm, is not negative ({{ F.g3b_child_c_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta }} {{ F.g3b_child_c_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_ci }} dB, an interval that also contains its fixed-helmet Δ). With the room field projected out, for which only the pooled medians are stored in this helmet, the difference of medians from the adult in its own scaled helmet is {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_proj_d_minus_adult_cfx_range }} dB ({{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_cfx_range }} dB with sensor and brain noise). Scaled about the centred head instead, the helmet leaves residual gains in the heads that sit off-centre, the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_months }}- and {{ F.g3b_infant18mo_age_months }}-month templates and child C ({{ F.g3b_infant2yr_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta }}, {{ F.g3b_infant18mo_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta }} and {{ F.g3b_child_c_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta }} dB; Δ {{ F.g3b_smaller_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB overall). The OPM's absolute advantage remains in the scaled helmet (D~child~ {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_range }} dB): what disappears is the additional child-adult contrast.

Three checks qualify this result ([@fig-fit]b, d). First, part of the drop comes from the reference: against the adult at top contact rather than in its own counterfactual helmet, the difference of medians is {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_top_range }} dB (child C {{ F.g3b_child_c_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_top }} dB, above its fixed-helmet Δ). Second, scaling the helmet also scales the gap, which shrinks below the adult's {{ F.g3b_adult_cfx_gap }} mm in {{ F.g3b_cfx_gap_below_adult_count }} of the {{ F.g4_n_smaller_heads }} smaller heads ({{ F.g3b_cfx_gap_below_adult_range }} mm). This helmet therefore fits most smaller heads more tightly than the adult's helmet fits the adult, which favours Neuromag, and by design it removes the sphere's head-size effect at a constant {{ F.lit_xi_squid_mm }}-mm gap, which is not tested here. Third, the smaller heads carry fewer OPM sites ({{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_sites_range }} against {{ F.g3b_adult_dense_sites }}): the adult's array subsampled to those counts loses {{ F.g3b_smaller_site_penalty_vs_combined_range }} dB, so at an equal site count Δ in the fixed helmet would be {{ F.g3b_smaller_eqcount_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB, and adding this loss back to the laterally centred counterfactual makes Δ positive in {{ F.g3b_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_plus_penalty_positive_count }} of the {{ F.g4_n_smaller_heads }} heads ({{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_plus_penalty_range }} dB, an additive estimate).

Depth is the route of head size that the sphere describes ([@fig-fit]e, f). The templates' and children's cortex is shallower than the adult's: area-weighted median depths of {{ F.g3b_templates_depth_wmedian_range }} mm (templates) and {{ F.g3b_children_depth_wmedian_range }} mm (children) against {{ F.g3b_adult_depth_wmedian }} mm, with {{ F.g3b_templates_area_share_10_20mm_pct_range }} and {{ F.g3b_children_area_share_10_20mm_pct_range }} of the cortical area {{ F.g3b_depth_span_10_20_mm }} mm below the scalp against {{ F.g3b_adult_area_share_10_20mm_pct }}. Reweighting each head's targets to the adult's depth distribution lowers the difference of pooled medians from the adult: for the templates from {{ F.g3b_templates_pooled_diff_range }} dB to {{ F.g3b_templates_reweighted_diff_range }} dB, for the children from {{ F.g3b_children_pooled_diff_range }} dB to {{ F.g3b_children_reweighted_diff_range }} dB. Within depth strata, where Δ is the difference of the two heads' area-weighted medians (Section 2.5), it is positive in every stratum of the scaled adults and templates ({{ F.g3b_templates_scaled_dense_vs_combined_ib_depth_delta_range }} dB). For the individual children it is negative in {{ F.g3b_children_depth_10_30mm_delta_negative_count }} of the {{ F.g3b_children_depth_10_30mm_cells_n }} child-strata of {{ F.g3b_depth_span_10_30_mm }} mm depth ({{ F.g3b_children_dense_vs_combined_ib_depth_10_30mm_delta_range }} dB; no interval lies above zero, the largest lower bound being {{ F.g3b_children_depth_10_30mm_ci_lo_max }} dB) and positive deeper ({{ F.g3b_children_dense_vs_combined_ib_depth_30_60mm_delta_range }} dB). In this decomposition the individual children's fixed-helmet gain is accounted for by their shallower cortex: their wider SQUID gap adds no net gain once depth is matched. The two decompositions, scaling the helmet and matching depth, are not additive, so the routes cannot be apportioned.

The noise differs between the classes under the convention that the background variance per unit cortical area is the adult's. In the fixed helmet, Neuromag's magnetometer brain noise falls from {{ F.g3b_adult_noise_top_mag_brain }} fT in the adult at top contact ({{ F.g3b_adult_noise_centred_mag_brain }} fT at its measured pose, as in Section 3.2) to {{ F.g3b_templates_scaled_noise_top_mag_brain_range }} fT in the templates and scaled adults, mainly because their gap is wider: moving the school-age size control from its centred pose up to top contact raises it from {{ F.g3b_school_noise_centred_mag_brain }} to {{ F.g3b_school_noise_top_mag_brain }} fT, and a smaller cortex with the same background per unit area does not lower the field at sensors that stay close, as the OPMs show ({{ F.g3b_school_noise_dense_brain }} and {{ F.g3b_size2yr_noise_dense_brain }} fT in the scaled adults against {{ F.g3b_adult_noise_dense_brain }} fT in the adult). The children's cortex is nearly adult-sized ({{ F.g3b_children_cortex_area_cm2_range }} cm^2^): Neuromag's brain noise stays at {{ F.g3b_children_noise_top_mag_brain_range }} fT, and the OPMs, close to a near-adult cortex that lies near the scalp, see more of it ({{ F.g3b_children_noise_dense_brain_range }} fT). These children's surfaces call for caution. Child B's head is the size of the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_months }}-month template's (head circumference {{ F.g3b_child_b_ofc_cm }} against {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_ofc_cm }} cm, cap volume {{ F.g3b_child_b_cap_volume_cm3 }} against {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_cap_volume_cm3 }} cm^3^) but carries {{ F.g3b_child_b_cortex_area_cm2 }} cm^2^ of cortex against {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_cortex_area_cm2 }} cm^2^, and the children's white surfaces come within {{ F.g3b_children_white_to_scalp_closest_range }} mm of the scalp. A scalp surface lying inside the true scalp, or misregistered relative to the white surface, would inflate both the OPMs' brain noise and the shallow-cortex effect, so the children's routes are provisional (Section 4.4). The share of cortex on which the OPM is ahead, the preprint's kind of metric, is saturated for the dense array ({{ F.g3b_adult_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_share_pos }} in the adult), while for the matched array it is {{ F.g3b_adult_matched_vs_combined_ib_d_share_pos }} in the adult at top contact (a share of cortical area, against {{ F.g2_matched_vs_combined_ib_share }} of all targets at the measured pose in Section 3.3) and {{ F.g3b_smaller_matched_vs_combined_ib_d_share_pos_range }} in the smaller heads in the fixed helmet ([@fig-fit]c).

Taken together, fit and head size act through opposing routes that this model cannot separate. In the fixed helmet the raising routes win in every smaller head: for the scaled adults and templates a gain remains at matched depth, so the wider gap contributes, whereas for the individual children their shallower cortex alone accounts for it. In a helmet scaled with the head about the laterally centred head the extra gain disappears, partly because that helmet also brings the SQUIDs closer than the adult's own fit. This is a statement about this model's conventions (the counterfactual construction, the background per unit area, one adult reference), not a general law of head size.

### 3.8 Brain regions across head sizes {#res-regions-heads}

*Which regions favour the OPM at which head size?* In the fixed helmet the dense array is ahead in every region of every head ([@fig-regions-heads]a; {{ F.regh_top_vs_combined_cells_range }} dB), and a smaller head exceeds the adult's value for the same region in {{ F.regh_top_vs_combined_smaller_cells_above_adult_count }} of {{ F.regh_top_vs_combined_smaller_cells_n }} cells. The cingulate is the lowest row in every head ({{ F.regh_adult_top_vs_combined_cingulate }} dB in the adult, {{ F.regh_smaller_top_vs_combined_cingulate_range }} dB in the smaller heads).

In the counterfactual helmet the regional gain is smaller, and how much smaller depends on the reference ([@fig-regions-heads]b, lobes only). Against the adult in its own scaled helmet, {{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_smaller_lobe_cells_at_or_below_adult_count }} of the {{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_smaller_lobe_cells_n }} lobe cells of the smaller heads are at or below the adult's; against the adult at top contact, {{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_smaller_lobe_cells_at_or_below_adult_top_count }} are ({{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_templates_scaled_lobe_cells_at_or_below_adult_top_count }} of {{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_templates_scaled_lobe_cells_n }} for the templates and scaled adults, {{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_children_lobe_cells_at_or_below_adult_top_count }} of {{ F.regh_cfx_vs_combined_children_lobe_cells_n }} for the children); these counts compare point estimates without a tolerance. Region by region, then, the answer depends on the helmet as much as on the region.

### 3.9 The noise floor across heads {#res-noise-heads}

*Does the OPM noise condition hold for children as well?* D falls with the OPM's sensor noise in every head ([@fig-noise-floor]). At {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz, about the empty-room floor read from the preprint's recording, the dense array's D is {{ F.g3b_adult_asd30_dense_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB in the adult and {{ F.g3b_smaller_asd30_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_range }} dB in the smaller heads; the matched array's is negative in every head except the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_years }}-year size control ({{ F.g3b_adult_asd30_matched_vs_combined_ib_d }} dB in the adult, {{ F.g3b_smaller_asd30_matched_vs_combined_ib_d_range }} dB in the smaller heads). The difference from the adult itself depends on the noise. At {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_min }} fT/√Hz the individual children are slightly below the adult ({{ F.g3b_children_asd7_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_range }} dB, difference of medians), at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz above it ({{ F.g3b_children_asd30_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_range }} dB). A plausible reading, not tested here, is that their OPM channels, dominated by brain noise, lose less to a noisier sensor than the adult's. A noisier OPM thus erases the adult's advantage first, while the smaller heads in the fixed helmet keep part of theirs. Detectability assumes a known source pattern in stationary noise; whether differences of this size change which interictal spikes a detector finds, without knowing source or time, is the last question.

## 4. Discussion {#discussion}

### 4.1 Answers to the four questions {#disc-answers}

**Q1: what replaces η.** With explicit noise, brain noise dominates every magnetometer channel, and the implied single-channel noise ratio OPM/magnetometer (about {{ F.g2_noise_rms_dense_vs_mag_noroom_ratio_1dp }} with sensor and brain noise) is somewhat above our reading of the preprint's spectra where brain activity dominates (about {{ F.lit_sef_midband_noise_ratio }}). That ratio is set by the brain noise each array sees and hardly depends on the OPM's floor ({{ F.g2_noise_rms_dense_vs_mag_noroom_asd7_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_min }} and {{ F.g2_noise_rms_dense_vs_mag_noroom_asd30_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz). The floor nevertheless decides the whole-array comparison: the dense array's detectability is {{ F.g2_sens_asd7ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} times Neuromag's at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_min }}, {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_primary }} and {{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz, a dependence that a single η cannot capture. What the model lacks, the measured OPM's own floor, therefore decides the comparison.

**Q2: brain regions.** With sensor and brain noise the dense array leads in every lobe, least in the cingulate and insula; among the temporal regions chosen from the epilepsy literature its lead is smallest in mesial temporal cortex, although {{ F.reg_dk_dense_vs_combined_ib_below_parahippocampal_count }} atlas parcels, mostly cingulate and medial, have smaller leads. The matched array falls behind on ventral and polar surfaces: in mesial temporal cortex, its lowest of the regions chosen, and more in orbitofrontal and polar cortex. Comparator and metric matter at least as much as region; Section 4.2 sets this against the clinical reports.

**Q3: head size or helmet fit.** With {{ F.g2_opm_asd_primary }}-fT/√Hz OPMs in the fixed adult helmet, D rises in every smaller head at top contact (Δ {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB), least in the individual children ({{ F.g3b_children_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB; {{ F.g3b_children_dense_vs_combined_proj_delta_range }} dB projected). A wider SQUID gap and a shallower cortex raise it; fewer OPM sites and, in the school-aged children, a near-adult cortex close to the OPMs lower it. Matching depth leaves a gain in the scaled adults and templates but none in the individual children, and a helmet scaled with the head about the laterally centred head removes the extra gain (Δ {{ F.g3b_smaller_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_range }} dB), in part because it brings the SQUIDs closer than the adult's fit; the routes act jointly and cannot be apportioned. Among the lobes, the fixed-helmet gain is largest in the temporal lobe of the infant templates ({{ F.regh_infant12mo_top_vs_combined_temporal }} dB at {{ F.g3b_infant12mo_age_months }} months against {{ F.regh_adult_top_vs_combined_temporal }} dB in the adult) and is smaller in the scaled helmet. With {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_min }}-fT/√Hz OPMs the individual children fall slightly below the adult ({{ F.g3b_children_asd7_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_minus_adult_range }} dB).

**Q4: interictal spikes.** In this model a dense array detects weak simulated spikes {{ F.g4_band_10_20mm }} mm below the scalp at a lower strength in every anatomy (point estimates, with per-location tests favouring it in each). Deeper spikes lean the same way less consistently, no localization difference is established, and an array at the SQUID sites shows no consistent advantage.

**Number of sensors.** A single-axis array at Neuromag's sites is level with Neuromag only with sensor and brain noise; a triaxial array there or a dense array leads; and subsampling the adult's dense array to the smaller heads' site counts costs {{ F.g3b_smaller_site_penalty_vs_combined_range }} dB on the adult head. Arrays of only a few sensors were not simulated.

### 4.2 Reconciling the clinical literature {#disc-literature}

The clinical studies and the sphere do not contradict each other once the comparator, metric, sensor count and noise are aligned ([@tab-literature]). We take as each study's model counterpart the quantity with its comparator and metric, with sensor and brain noise, in the adult or, for pediatric cohorts, in the smaller heads; where a study has no SQUID comparator, the counterpart is a contrast within one system. Three patterns in the literature have counterparts in the model, and the reconciliation concerns direction, not magnitude.

First, **against magnetometers, peak-channel SNR favours OPMs.** Against the SQUID system's 102 magnetometers, OPM SNR was higher in four of five children (Feys et al., 2022) and in one of three tested patients (Feys et al., 2025). In the model the dense array's peak-channel SNR against the magnetometers is {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_mag_ib_ratio }} in the adult, and D for this metric is {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_vs_mag_ib_peak_d_range }} dB in the smaller heads in the fixed helmet against {{ F.g3b_adult_dense_vs_mag_ib_peak_d }} dB in the adult at top contact: the OPM ahead of the magnetometers, as in four of the five children of Feys et al. (2022), a study without an adult comparison.

Second, **against a full gradiometer array, or with noisier sensors, the SQUID is equal or ahead.** Six dual-axis sensors against the planar gradiometers of a 306-channel MEGIN Triux Neo (Hillebrand et al., 2023) and four helium-4 sensors of below 45 fT/√Hz against the nearest channels of a commercial 275-channel CTF system (Schwartz et al., 2025) gave equal or slightly higher SQUID SNR. In the model the dense array's peak-channel SNR against Neuromag's gradiometers is {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_grad_ib_ratio }} {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_grad_ib_ci }} (matched {{ F.g2_peak_matched_vs_grad_ib_ratio }}), within the range of Hillebrand's derived ratios (0.92 to 0.98), and against the best of all {{ F.g2_squid_channels }} channels {{ F.g2_peak_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}; with {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }}-fT/√Hz OPMs the dense array's detectability ratio is {{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }} and the matched array's {{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio_3dp }}. The model has no array of only a few sensors, so the smallest clinical arrays have no direct counterpart. Amplitude, by contrast, is always larger with OPMs, in the literature (2.3 to 4.6 times in Feys et al., 2022; 1.4 to 5.3 times in the individual patients of Schwartz et al., 2025) and in the model's peak-field ratios ({{ F.g2_leadfield_dense_vs_mag_bins_range_1dp }} times, depending on depth).

Third, **among temporal sources, mesial temporal ones give the weakest OPM results.** In the model the dense array's lead is smallest there among the temporal regions examined, and the matched array falls furthest behind there among the regions chosen from the literature (Section 3.5). Only Feys et al. (2025) compared OPM and SQUID SNR within patients by region: its two mesial temporal patients had nominally lower OPM SNR, as did the deep, SEEG-confirmed spike of Schwartz et al. (2025). Feys et al. (2024) and Shen et al. (2026), which had no SQUID comparator, show that mesial temporal sources are hard for OPM-MEG in absolute terms, which the model gives for both systems alike: the dense array's own detectability in the parahippocampal gyrus is {{ F.reg_parahippocampal_vs_superiortemporal_dense_abs_ratio }} of that in the superior temporal gyrus, and Neuromag's {{ F.reg_parahippocampal_vs_superiortemporal_combined_abs_ratio }}. Westin et al. (2023) found the on-scalp advantage preserved for mesial temporal spikes, visible on scalp from 0.5 against 1.54 cm^2^ with conventional MEG, although the smallest mesial temporal seizure-onset patch was the only one not visible on scalp.

The clinical SNRs are sensor-level ratios of real or averaged discharges, often in wider bands and with few sensors, against different SQUID systems; the model computes ratios of the same kinds for single cortical dipoles in {{ F.g2_band_range_hz }} Hz, on surface sources only, without face sensors or non-brain physiological noise, and for one adult. Matsubara et al. (2026), with brain noise only, OPMs at SQUID-matched positions and a channel-averaged SNR, found no regional SNR gain; with the same metric the model gives neither array a clear lead (dense {{ F.g2_meanpow_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}, matched {{ F.g2_meanpow_matched_vs_combined_ib_ratio }}), so the dense array's lead appears only with the whitened detectability metric. In the only cohort comparison of detection, IED detection did not differ significantly between the systems (Ren et al., 2025), consistent with a model gain that is confined to weak spikes, whose share in recordings is unknown.

### 4.3 Implications for pediatric epilepsy and for system design {#disc-implications}

For a child in an adult helmet, the model supports a modest advantage of a dense, low-noise OPM array for detecting weak superficial spikes with a whitened multichannel detector: in every anatomy the point estimate of its strength for {{ F.g4_s50_level }} detection {{ F.g4_band_10_20mm }} mm below the scalp was {{ F.g4_all_dense_vs_combined_practical_10_20mm_reduction_pct_range }} lower than Neuromag's. The model does not show that this advantage is larger in children. Detectability rises in every smaller head in the adult helmet, but the S50 ratios of the individual children ({{ F.g4_childa_dense_vs_combined_practical_10_20mm_ratio }}, {{ F.g4_childb_dense_vs_combined_practical_10_20mm_ratio }} and {{ F.g4_childc_dense_vs_combined_practical_10_20mm_ratio }}) lie on both sides of the adult's ({{ F.g4_adult_dense_vs_combined_practical_10_20mm_ratio }}), the adult was simulated at its measured pose rather than at top contact, and the spike study did not use the counterfactual helmet. By peak-channel SNR, closer to channel-based review, Neuromag's best channel leads in the adult (D {{ F.g3b_adult_dense_vs_combined_ib_peak_d }} dB at top contact) and in {{ F.g3b_smaller_dense_vs_combined_ib_peak_d_negative_count }} of the {{ F.g4_n_smaller_heads }} smaller heads. Nor does the model support a general rule that smaller heads favour OPMs: in a helmet scaled with the head the extra gain disappears for the templates and scaled adults, while for the individual children the result depends on the construction and the reference (child C {{ F.g3b_child_c_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta }} {{ F.g3b_child_c_cfx_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta_ci }} dB about the laterally centred head, {{ F.g3b_child_c_cf_dense_vs_combined_ib_delta }} dB about the centred head), and at matched depth the individual school-aged children are not better off than the adult. The relevant comparison for a pediatric centre is therefore an OPM system against the SQUID system the child would actually be measured with; dedicated pediatric SQUID systems (Johnson et al., 2010; Roberts et al., 2014; Okada et al., 2016) are the real counterfactual that our geometric construction only approximates.

For system design the model gives three levers. The OPM noise floor in the band of interest decides whether there is an advantage at all ({{ F.g2_sens_asd7ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_min }} fT/√Hz, {{ F.g2_sens_asd30ft_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz). Array density and coverage decide how large it is. And fit matters for both systems: a {{ F.g2_opm_gap_large_mm }}-mm gap between scalp and OPMs lowers the dense array's ratio by {{ F.g2_gap6mm_dense_cost_pct }}, as a poor SQUID fit costs Neuromag. Coverage below the temporal lobe, not simulated here, might matter most for ventromedial temporal cortex, and face-mounted OPMs have been tried for it (Feys et al., 2025).

### 4.4 Limitations {#disc-limitations}

**Noise realism.** The OPM noise is white and declared, without the floor and low-frequency component of measured sensors and without cardiac, ocular or muscle fields; the OPM covariance is validated by nothing; and a single projection replaces the preprocessing of real systems (signal-space separation for Neuromag, Taulu & Kajola, 2005; Taulu & Simola, 2006; homogeneous-field correction for OPMs, Tierney et al., 2021). The cortical background under-predicts the measured magnetometer brain noise, part of which is likely non-cortical, and, like the targets, it omits the cortex within {{ F.g2_source_skull_clearance_mm }} mm of the inner skull, whose effect on the OPM/Neuromag balance was not estimated. The noise sweep bounds a uniformly worse sensor, not a coloured one.

**One adult reference.** Every Δ uses one adult anatomy, whose D varies by {{ F.g3b_adult_family_dense_vs_combined_ib_d_span }} dB over its standard placements, and the spike study's adult sits at its measured pose rather than at top contact.

**Anatomy of the smaller heads.** The scaled adults are copies of the adult, the templates are averages with smooth cortices, and the children are individuals of one dataset with a modelled skull and transferred fiducials. Their white surfaces come within {{ F.g3b_children_white_to_scalp_closest_range }} mm of the scalp (child B has {{ F.g3b_child_b_shallow_targets_lt10mm }} targets shallower than {{ F.g3b_shallow_target_limit_mm }} mm, the adult {{ F.g3b_adult_shallow_targets_lt10mm }}), and child B's head is the size of the {{ F.g3b_infant2yr_age_months }}-month template's while its cortex is much larger. These point to a scalp surface lying inside the true scalp or misregistered relative to the white surface, which would inflate the children's OPM brain noise and their shallow-cortex effect; the surfaces should be checked against the MRIs before the children's routes are relied on. The background per unit cortical area gives the templates less background by construction; age-specific physiology is not modelled beyond a bounded scaling.

**Sources.** All sources lie on the cortical surface; hippocampus and amygdala, the generators of many mesial temporal discharges, are absent, so the mesial temporal conclusions rest on parahippocampal and entorhinal proxies, whose depth to the nearest scalp point understates their distance from the sensors.

**Detection and localization.** The practical detector's templates are the simulated morphologies and its candidates come from the true forward model, which makes it optimistic for every array, whether equally so was not tested; it is a whitened matched filter, the same principle as the detectability metric, so the agreement between the two is partly by construction, whereas channel-based clinical review is closer to peak-channel SNR. The null data contain no physiological transients; frozen thresholds left unequal operating points ({{ F.g4_heldout_rate_range }} false events per minute held out), and the rate-matched check fits and evaluates thresholds on the same held-out data; the locations are frontal-heavy ({{ F.g4_adult_locations_frontal }} frontal against {{ F.g4_adult_locations_occipital }} occipital in the adult), and each band has {{ F.g4_locations_per_band }} locations and one noise realization. Localization is evaluated at the true peak sample with one coregistration draw per location.

**Statistics and model history.** The endpoints and the regions were chosen after the analyses, only the spike endpoint is corrected for multiplicity and only across anatomies, Δ has no test or margin, and the model was revised after the pediatric results were known.

**Motion.** Head motion is bounded rather than modelled ([S4](epilepsy.html)): a sustained {{ F.mot_translation_largest_mm }}-mm drop in the fixed helmet costs Neuromag {{ F.mot_squid_down10mm_mismatched_loss_db_range }} dB uncompensated and {{ F.mot_squid_down10mm_known_loss_db_range }} dB with the displaced geometry known, the latter comparable to an uncompensated {{ F.mot_slip_largest_deg }}° slip of the OPM cap ({{ F.mot_opm_slip3deg_mismatched_loss_db_range }} dB).

### 4.5 Future work {#disc-future}

Four steps would most strengthen these conclusions: a measured OPM noise model, with its floor, low-frequency and physiological components, carried through every analysis as a co-primary condition; more adults with recorded helmet positions and more children with individual MRIs, to estimate variability between subjects; a sweep of head circumference with a fitted counterfactual helmet at every size, at the adult's gap as well as at a scaled one, and of OPM sensor count; and volume sources in hippocampus and amygdala, face-mounted sensors and the geometry of real pediatric SQUID systems, so that the mesial temporal question can be asked directly.

## 5. Conclusions {#conclusions}

With explicit sensor, cortical-background and room noise on a realistic head in place of an assumed noise ratio, a dense on-scalp OPM array detects cortical sources modestly better than the {{ F.g2_squid_channels }}-channel Neuromag system ({{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_ib_ratio }} times with sensor and brain noise, {{ F.g2_dense_vs_combined_proj_ratio }} projected), mostly superficial ones, whereas an array at the SQUID sites is level with Neuromag only with sensor and brain noise, noisier sensors lose, and peak-channel SNR against the full SQUID system favours the SQUID. The dense lead is smallest in cingulate cortex and, among the temporal regions examined, in mesial temporal cortex, where an array at the SQUID sites falls behind. These conditions reconcile the spherical model with the clinical reports in direction. Smaller heads gain more in an adult helmet, through a wider SQUID gap and a shallower cortex that this model cannot separate (in the individual children the shallower cortex alone accounts for it); a helmet scaled with the head, gaps included, removes the extra gain when it is centred laterally. For simulated interictal spikes the advantage is a lower detection threshold for weak superficial spikes in every anatomy (point estimates), without an established difference in localization. All of this holds for a white OPM noise of {{ F.g2_opm_asd_primary }} fT/√Hz; at {{ F.g2_opm_asd_sweep_max }} fT/√Hz the adult advantage disappears.
