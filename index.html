Brain region, head size and helmet fit in on-scalp versus SQUID MEG: a simulation study from adults to children, with simulated interictal spikes

Laiba Khan

Highlights

  • In the tested conditions, OPM noise or array choice can reverse which system leads
  • OPM noise is unmeasured, and the model errs for Neuromag by as much as the dense lead
  • In the model a dense OPM array leads Neuromag; a site-matched one shows no clear lead
  • Fitting the helmet to the adult's median gap shrinks the smaller heads' extra lead
  • With an idealized detector, dense OPMs detected weaker shallow simulated spikes

Abstract

On-scalp optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) record larger fields than cryogenic SQUID magnetoencephalography (MEG), but in a spherical model they lead only for sources shallower than a depth set by an assumed noise ratio. We replaced that ratio with explicit sensor, brain and room noise, compared the Neuromag system with a dense and a site-matched OPM array by known-topography detectability in an adult and eight smaller heads, and simulated interictal spikes. In the adult the lead depended on the OPM noise floor and the array: with sensor plus brain noise a dense 15-fT/√Hz array had 1.14 [1.12, 1.17] times Neuromag's detectability (interval: spatial variation within this head), a lead not established at 30 fT/√Hz, and the site-matched array showed no established advantage. The dense lead survived the omissions tested but is no larger than the model's error for Neuromag (assumptions about each system's real noise give 0.80 to 1.14): a conditional prediction until OPM noise is measured. Under the modelled noise (exploratory), the dense lead was smallest in cingulate cortex and the site-matched array weakest on ventral and polar surfaces. In the fixed adult helmet the scaled adults' and infant templates' lead exceeded the adult's by +0.44 to +1.07 dB; with the helmet fitted to the adult's median gap at top contact, +0.10 to +0.23 dB of it remained. The school-aged children, whose near-scalp anatomy could not be verified, are provisional examples. In a confirmatory run (modelled noise, idealized detector, fixed adult helmet), the dense array detected simulated spikes 10–20 mm deep at 21% to 32% lower strength than Neuromag in all nine anatomies (not independent replications); no localization difference was established (exploratory). Clinical reports of higher OPM SNR are compatible with these conditions but not explained by them.

Keywords: magnetoencephalography; optically pumped magnetometers; pediatric MEG; helmet fit; noise modelling; interictal spikes

1. Introduction

Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) measure the brain's magnetic field at room temperature and can be worn on the scalp, whereas the superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) of conventional magnetoencephalography (MEG) sit in a rigid cryogenic helmet (Boto et al., 2017; Iivanainen et al., 2017). The helmet is built for adults: a child's head sits in it with a wide and uneven gap, whereas an on-scalp array fits any head (Hill et al., 2019; Feys & De Tiège, 2024). Clinical OPM studies of epilepsy report larger interictal discharges than cryogenic MEG (Feys et al., 2022). Their signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) are higher in some comparisons (Feys et al., 2022, 2025; Ren et al., 2025, abstract only) and equal or slightly lower in others (Hillebrand et al., 2023; Schwartz et al., 2025), and their weakest results concern temporal and mesial temporal sources (Feys et al., 2024, 2025; Shen et al., 2026).

A larger field does not by itself give a higher SNR. Jas et al. (2026) showed this in a spherical head with a point magnetometer on the scalp (OPM) and 18 mm above it (SQUID): if the OPM's noise is η times the SQUID's, its single-channel SNR is higher only for sources shallower than an equal-SNR depth, about 28 mm at η = 3 in an adult. With a SQUID shell that follows the head at a constant standoff, that depth covers a larger fraction of a small brain, a predicted benefit for children.

A realistic comparison needs explicit noise seen through each array's own lead fields, finite arrays on a real scalp and a real helmet around a smaller head. With sensor noise only, a helium-4 OPM array's advantage over SQUID magnetometers grew as the head shrank, in a standard adult placement and in a helmet fitted at a constant gap (Zahran et al., 2022). With brain noise on one adult, simulated spikes were visible on the scalp from smaller cortical patches than with conventional MEG (Westin et al., 2023). With brain noise but no sensor noise, OPMs at the SQUID sites of epilepsy patients raised a channel-averaged SNR in no region, though the OPM–SQUID difference correlated with the sensor-distance ratio, which followed head circumference (Matsubara et al., 2026). None compared head size and helmet fit with brain noise, fitted a helmet at the adult's own median gap (Zahran et al.'s constant gap is their adult's closest distance), or checked its noise model against a measured covariance; this study does all three, although its fitted helmet changes gap, placement and coverage together.

We therefore ask four questions. (Q1) What decides the comparison between OPM arrays and the Neuromag system on a real adult head with explicit noise, and how far can the noise model be trusted? (Q2) Which brain regions favour the OPM, and where does a site-matched array fall behind? (Q3) Is the larger relative advantage expected in smaller heads a matter of head size or of the fixed adult helmet? (Q4) Does a detectability difference change which simulated interictal spikes are detected, and how well they are localized?

2. Methods

2.1 Study design and estimands

The primary metric is known-topography detectability, d = √(sᵀC⁻¹s): the output SNR of the matched filter for a source of known field pattern s in noise of covariance C, with the true (oracle) covariance. Arrays are compared target by target by the paired ratio dOPM/dNeuromag, or by D = 20 log10 of it in dB, positive when the OPM is ahead. For the adult we report the unweighted median over all 7,661 targets, the 558 on the medial wall included, as a ratio (the medial wall is not cortex; without it the dense array's ratio is 1.148). For the smaller heads, whose cortices differ in size, we report D as an area-weighted median without the medial wall, and Δ = Dhead − Dadult with the adult placed by the same helmet rule. 2.2 Anatomies

The adult is the MNE sample subject (Gramfort et al., 2013) with FreeSurfer surfaces and atlas labels (Fischl, 2012; Desikan et al., 2006). Targets are the usable vertices of an oct-6 source space on the white surface at least 4 mm inside the inner skull, each a 10-nAm dipole normal to the cortex; depth is the distance to the nearest point of the MRI scalp; the three-layer boundary-element model (BEM; Hämäläinen & Sarvas, 1989; conductivities 0.3, 0.006, 0.3 S/m) has its outer surface on the MRI scalp.

The eight smaller heads fall into three classes (Table 2): two scaled adults (copies of the adult scaled to school-age size and to the 24-month template's head circumference), three infant templates at 24, 18 and 12 months (averages of many MRIs; O'Reilly et al., 2021; Richards et al., 2016) and three typically developing school-aged children, A, B and C (7.8 to 8.7 years; OpenNeuro ds005234, Fadeev et al., 2024, 2025). The classes are not independent subjects: two are copies of the adult, three are averages and three come from one dataset. All heads use the adult's conductivities; a single-compartment BEM changes each smaller head's median D minus the adult's by −0.06 to +0.01 dB.

The children's anatomy needed three steps (S1, section D):

  • Skull. The dataset's own BEM surfaces put the inner skull implausibly close to the scalp, a median 0.8 to 2.5 mm below it over the upper head. It was moved inward toward 8 mm below the scalp wherever it lay shallower, but kept at least 2 mm from every white-surface vertex, so that over the upper head it lies a median 5.7 to 7.8 mm below the scalp (adult 9.7 mm).
  • Fiducials. None come with the data. They were transferred from the adult by a similarity transform fitted between the adult's and the child's white surfaces (trimmed iterative closest points) and moved to the nearest scalp vertex, a method that reproduces the infant templates' own fiducials with errors of 1.6 to 14.4 mm and their head frames with rotations of 3.2 to 5.9°.
  • MRI check (Figure S6). Because their white surfaces come within 3.7 to 5.7 mm of the scalp, the surfaces were checked against the MRIs. The FreeSurfer scalp lies about as far inside the T1 head boundary in the children (median 1.3 to 1.5 mm) as in the adult (1.3 mm), and the white surfaces are registered to the T1, so, relative to the T1 images, the shallow cortex is not a scalp-placement or registration error.

The check cannot verify the white surface within a few millimetres of the scalp or the transferred fiducials, nor detect a head boundary that the T1 images themselves place too deep, which could explain both the shallow cortex and the dataset's inner skull lying so close to the scalp (S1, section D). The count of shallow targets depends on the scalp definition (measured to the MRI head boundary, child B has 47 instead of 247 targets less than 10 mm deep), and the source rule leaves out 3.3% to 10.0% of the children's cortex (adult 7.2%), including 94% to 100% of their cortex within 8 mm of the scalp. The children's results are therefore provisional geometric examples, not evidence for a mechanism.

The infant templates are averages. The same check puts their scalps 1.3 to 1.6 mm outside their own MRI head boundaries, where the adult's lies inside, so that relative to that boundary both systems' sensors sit farther from a template's brain than from the adult's, which may lower the templates' D and Δ. The check's rules also class the 18-month template as misregistered: its white surface fits its T1 best after a 2.3-mm shift (0.5 mm in the other two templates; Section 4.3).

2.3 Arrays and helmets

Neuromag (306 channels) combines the sample recording's VectorView geometry, MNE-Python's T3 coil definitions (types 3014 and 3024) and the TRIUX datasheet's typical white noise, 3.5 fT/√Hz and 3.6 fT/(cm √Hz), with its 18-mm Dewar spacing between coils and helmet (Elekta Neuromag, n.d.); no signal-space separation is applied.

OPM arrays (Figure 1a to c). Each OPM is a single-axis sensor with its sensing centre nominally 7 mm above the MRI scalp (Osborne et al., 2018; Alem et al., 2023; a few sites moved outward for clearance) and a 10-mm cubic averaging volume, a modelling choice that changes the peak field by at most 1.9% (S7). Its axis is the head-surface normal averaged within 15 mm of the site (nearly radial axes give a projected dense ratio of 1.073 instead of 1.115; S1, section C). The OPM coverage region is the scalp above the plane through the preauricular points and a point 30 mm above the nasion, at least 20 mm from either preauricular point. The dense OPM array fills it at a minimum spacing of 17 mm (208 sites on the adult, more sites than any clinical OPM array), and the site-matched OPM array puts one OPM at each Neuromag site whose projection onto the scalp falls in it (98 of 102 sites; the other 4 fall outside it). On every head the dense array is rebuilt by these rules (144 to 174 sites on the smaller heads) and the site-matched array takes that head's Neuromag sites at top contact (80 to 91 sites; Table 2). Clinical systems reach about 100 to 128 channels with dual- or triaxial sensors at fewer sites (Feys et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026); such arrays are not modelled, and a triaxial array at the site-matched sites (294 channels) is a control for the gain from extra axes, not a model of them. The OPM white noise is 15 fT/√Hz in the primary analysis, flat over 1 to 40 Hz, and is swept over 7, 10, 15, 20 and 30 fT/√Hz (the clinical noise figures state no band; S7).

The fixed adult helmet (Figure 1d) keeps the adult's sensors and noise for every head. The primary placement is top contact: from the pose that puts the head's frame where the adult's was recorded, the head is raised until the nearest magnetometer coil is 20 mm from the scalp, a best-fit assumption that favours Neuromag (Gaetz et al., 2008; Tesan et al., 2010; S1, section E). A family of 12 source-blind placements, chosen from the geometry alone (contacts, shifts and rotations; S1, section E), gives the placement band.

Helmet fitted at the adult's gap. Each head is laterally centred (moved sideways to the helmet's midline), and the helmet scaled about the head origin until the median magnetometer-to-scalp gap equals the adult's in the same pose, 29.6 mm (the adult's gap is 29.8 mm at its measured head position and 28.4 mm at top contact). No coil may come within the 18-mm Dewar spacing of the scalp; in child C this limit binds first, at 32.8 mm. The helmet scaled with the head, which scales the gap too, is a secondary control (S1, section E), and a control at equal OPM site count subsamples the adult's dense array to each smaller head's site count. None models a pediatric SQUID device.

2.4 Noise model, validation and sensitivity analyses

Every array sees identical noise sources in one band of 1 to 40 Hz. Each array's oracle covariance is computed in closed form as C = Σs + s² Lbg A LbgT + E Σe ET, where Σs is the diagonal white sensor noise, Lbg holds the lead fields of 1,755 independent cortex-normal background dipoles on a 7-mm grid, A is the diagonal matrix of their Voronoi-cell areas, and E and Σe are the array's responses to 8 external-field terms and their coefficient covariance. Each background dipole's moment variance is s² times its area (s² = 0.0354 nAm² per mm²); like the targets, the background omits the cortex within 4 mm of the inner skull. Two quantities are fitted to the sample dataset: Σe (to the empty-room recording) and the scalar s², so that the median good gradiometer's modelled brain noise equals the measured one (an RMS of 37.1 fT/cm). Everything else is a prediction, except the declared sensor noise of both systems. The conditions are sensor noise only; sensor plus brain noise (primary); sensor, brain and room noise; and the last with the room field's subspace removed from every array by one noise-weighted projection ('projected', the second condition named before any simulation; S1, section H).

Validation. The model's Neuromag covariance was compared with the measured one of the same recording, and Neuromag's detectability recomputed with it (S1, section C); as the OPM's covariance cannot be measured here, the implied OPM/Neuromag ratio was computed under five stated assumptions (scenarios A to E; Section 3.2).

Sensitivity analyses, each changing one element of the adult model and recalibrating s²: cortex 2 to 4 mm from the inner skull in the background, as targets, or both; coloured OPM noise (1/f plus white, corners at 1, 3 and 10 Hz) under a matched filter that also whitens across frequency; idealized cardiac and ocular far-field sources; and all together.

2.5 Simulated interictal spikes

The spike study runs the noise model in the time domain, in segments shared by all arrays, with the spike-wave complex of Hunold et al. (2016) stretched to three morphologies as the spike. Two matched-filter detectors read the data, both whitened with a Ledoit–Wolf covariance (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004) estimated from 10 min of null data rather than with the oracle covariance of Section 2.4: the oracle knows source and time and tests at a per-trial false-positive probability of 0.001; the practical detector knows neither, scans candidate patterns on a 10-mm grid without the true sources, and has thresholds for a nominal 1 false event per minute frozen on independent null data. Both are idealized: they use the true forward model and the simulated waveform family. S50, the strength for 50% detection, is interpolated linearly in log strength between the tested strengths.

Exploratory design. Spikes were placed at 72 locations stratified by depth (four bands from 10–20 to 45–70 mm, 18 locations each) and orientation, each with 18 focal events of 10 to 320 nAm and patch events, in one noise realization per event, identical in every array. In this and the confirmatory run the adult sits at its measured head position (D 0.28 dB above top contact; Table 1 and Section 3.4), the smaller heads at top contact; every comparison is within one anatomy.

Confirmatory design, fixed before the run. The design was declared in configs/g4_confirmatory.toml, committed to the repository on 4 October 2026 (commit 10e37b9) before the run, which ran on 5 October at commit ae458a9 (the same code and configuration); both are attested by the repository's history alone (Data and code availability), and the run had no external registration. The endpoint is the dense OPM array against Neuromag (306 channels) with the practical detector at a nominal 1 false event per minute (a threshold set on 20 min of calibration null rests on about 20 false events, a Poisson uncertainty of about 22%), for focal spikes 10–20 mm below the scalp in all nine anatomies: the two-sided sign-flip test on per-location differences in detection counts (p by exact enumeration, computed after the run from the stored differences; the run's declared Monte Carlo estimate beyond 20 non-zero differences gives the same decisions in all 25 families), Holm-adjusted over the anatomies (Holm, 1979) at α = 0.05, with the paired S50 ratio r as the effect size (the OPM's 50% detection strength is lower by 1 − 1/r). The run draws 36 new locations per anatomy and five noise replicates per event, the first carrying the endpoint, and uses separate null data for threshold fitting and rate evaluation. The 36 locations double the exploratory 18: two pilot runs on test seeds in child A (8/1 and 9/4 locations favouring the OPM : Neuromag, p = 0.074 and 0.21; recorded in the declaration, their outputs not kept; S1, section G) prompted the doubling but do not estimate power. Secondary analyses: a declared mismatched detector (off-template morphologies, a single-compartment BEM, a shared coregistration error of 2 mm and 2°), the site-matched array, the oracle and rate-matched thresholds (re-fitted on held-out null data).

Localization, in the exploratory run, used equivalent-current-dipole fits and dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM; Dale et al., 2000) at the true peak sample on 24 locations, through a single-compartment BEM with a coregistration error of 2 mm and 2° (S1, section G).

2.6 Statistics

Neighbouring targets are correlated, so intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over the adult's 70 atlas labels or over each smaller head's parcels (resample counts: Table S15); they describe spatial variation within one anatomy. In the spike study the location is the unit of resampling and testing. The exploratory spike endpoint (chosen from 24 comparisons per OPM array), the primary helmet placement and the regions were named after the whole-cortex results were known, and the model was revised while results were known (S1, section H); only the confirmatory run carries a pre-specified test.

2.7 Software, data and code

Simulations used MNE-Python 1.13.2 (Gramfort et al., 2013) and NumPy 2.5.3, with the seeds listed in S1, section I.

3. Results

3.1 What decides the adult comparison

With explicit noise, which array detects a cortical source better, and what decides it? With sensor noise alone Neuromag leads both OPM arrays, the median paired ratio over the 7,661 targets being 0.74 (dense) and 0.53 (site-matched; Figure 2). With the room field included, brain noise is 92% of a dense-array OPM channel's in-band variance and 74% of a Neuromag magnetometer's (room field 25%). With sensor plus brain noise the dense array's ratio is 1.144 [1.121, 1.168], D = +1.17 dB, and the OPM is ahead at 7,657 of the 7,661 targets. The site-matched array's ratio is 1.009 [0.994, 1.027], D = +0.08 dB: no established advantage.

The comparator: against the 204 gradiometers alone the ratios are 1.33 and 1.13 (dense, site-matched), against the 102 magnetometers 1.19 and 1.04. The metric: by peak-channel SNR, the closest analogue of clinical sensor-level SNRs (whose baselines also hold physiological noise; S1, section A), the dense array is ahead of the magnetometers (1.23 [1.17, 1.27]) but behind the best of all 306 channels (0.90 [0.88, 0.93]), and by mean-power SNR its lead is small (1.04). The OPM noise floor: from 7 to 30 fT/√Hz the dense ratio falls from 1.298 to 1.008 [0.993, 1.027], no established advantage, and the site-matched ratio from 1.084 to 0.933; the break-even white noise is 31.5 [28.5, 34.9] fT/√Hz (dense) and 16.7 [14.0, 19.4] fT/√Hz (site-matched; Figure 3d).

Over the ranges tested, the other single changes move the dense ratio less (Figure 2). Projecting out the room field gives 1.115 [1.081, 1.149], and the site-matched array then trails Neuromag (0.948 [0.899, 0.977]). A 3- or 6-mm scalp gap lowers the dense ratio to 1.09 or 1.04, and 30 fT/√Hz with a 3-mm gap reverses it (0.968 [0.956, 0.978]). A triaxial array at the site-matched sites recovers much of the lead (1.12). With a covariance estimated from 4,680 independent samples, the declared scenario equivalent in time and bandwidth to 60 s of data, the ratios are 1.16 [1.14, 1.18] and 1.03 [1.01, 1.05]. A 3–70 Hz band changes little (1.137 and 1.011). Across these conditions, then, the OPM noise floor or the array can reverse which system leads, the comparator and the metric decide which reported number shows it, and depth changes the size of the dense lead and the sign of the site-matched array's (Section 3.3).

3.2 How far the noise model can be trusted

What does the model get right about Neuromag's noise, and what does that imply for the OPM? The model predicts 0.73 of the magnetometers' measured brain-noise amplitude, an independent prediction (Section 2.4; Figure S2), or 0.89 with the heart's field removed. The gradiometer level at randomly held-out sites (1.00; [0.93, 1.09] over 1,000 splits) and the overall pattern of channel correlations agree, the former nearly by construction. The modelled brain noise is, however, too flat from bottom to top (1.34 and 0.75 when fitted on the inferior or superior half), its magnetometer correlations and per-channel variances are poorly predicted, and the magnetometers' excess over the model sits in a few far-field components (S1, section C). For detectability this excess favours Neuromag: with its measured covariance Neuromag's detectability is 1.14 [1.08, 1.20] times its modelled value (Figure 3a), a factor as large as the dense array's whole lead.

What this implies for the OPM depends on its real noise, which no measurement here constrains (Figure 3b): if the model's error is common to both systems (scenario A), the ratios stay at 1.14 and 1.01, and nearly so if both cortical backgrounds are scaled by the magnetometers' variance excess over the model, a factor of 1.86 (scenario B), or if Neuromag's measured empty-room noise is used with the modelled brain noise (scenario E; S1, section C); if Neuromag is taken as measured and the OPM as modelled (scenario D), the dense ratio is 1.03 [0.99, 1.08], no established advantage, and the site-matched 0.89; if the OPM bears the whole magnetometer variance excess as cortical noise (scenario C, the most pessimistic), 0.80 and 0.68.

The omissions tested move the lead little at 15 fT/√Hz (Figure 3c; Table S4): cortex 2 to 4 mm from the inner skull gives 1.137 (as background) to 1.152 (as targets); a 1/f component cornering at 10 Hz gives 1.072 under the primary detector and 1.083 under one that also whitens across frequency. Cardiac and ocular sources change the dense ratio by −1.6% to +1.6%. The joint run of near-skull background with the 10-Hz corner, frequency-whitened (Figure 3d, dashed), gives 1.080 [1.058, 1.106] at 15, 1.016 [0.997, 1.035] at 20 and 0.935 at 30 fT/√Hz: under it the lead is established up to 15 but not 20 fT/√Hz, against up to 20 (1.083 [1.062, 1.105]) but not 30 fT/√Hz under the primary model (Section 3.1). A measured OPM covariance is the missing test.

3.3 Depth and regions

Where on the cortex does the dense array lead, and where does the site-matched array fall behind? The lead is largest near the scalp (Figure 4a): the dense ratio is 1.60 [1.55, 1.65] in the 10–15 mm bin, 1.13 [1.12, 1.14] at 25–30 mm and 1.05 to 1.07 from 35–40 to 55–60 mm, every interval above 1. The site-matched array has an equal-SNR depth even with sensor plus brain noise: its intervals lie above 1 down to the 25–30 mm bin and below 1 from 40–45 mm. With the room field projected out, the dense array's bin median falls below 1 from 45–50 mm and the site-matched array's from 25–30 mm (intervals below 1 only at 55–60 mm and from 30–35 mm, respectively). For the dense array the fall is geometric: it is present with sensor noise alone (Figure 4a, dotted) and in the peak field itself (Figure 4b), brain noise multiplying the ratio by a nearly constant factor in every bin (1.51 to 1.68). On the cortex (Figure 4c) the dense array is ahead almost everywhere, most on the lateral convexity.

By region (Figure 5) the dense lead is largest in the frontal lobe (1.22 [1.18, 1.26]) and smallest in the cingulate (1.05 [1.04, 1.07]) and the insula (1.09). Of the regions chosen from the epilepsy literature, the precentral gyrus favours it most (1.21) and the parahippocampal cortex least (1.12), though 12 atlas parcels, mostly cingulate and medial, have smaller leads. The site-matched array trails most on ventral and polar surfaces, e.g. the frontal pole (0.71), medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex (0.78 and 0.79) and the temporal pole (0.83), and, of the literature's regions, in mesial temporal cortex (parahippocampal and entorhinal; 0.92). These rankings are exploratory point estimates of the model, conditional on the OPM's unmeasured noise: under scenario D (Section 3.2; Neuromag as measured, the OPM as modelled) the dense ratio becomes 0.75 in the cingulate, 0.96 in the parietal, 1.01 in the frontal and 1.32 in the temporal lobe (point estimates), and falls below 1 from the 25–30 mm bin (room field included).

3.4 Smaller heads in the fixed adult helmet

Does a smaller head in the adult helmet change the OPM's relative performance? At top contact the median magnetometer-to-scalp gap is 32.6 to 40.4 mm in the smaller heads against 28.4 mm in the adult (Table 2). The dense array's D is +1.19 to +2.13 dB in the smaller heads and +1.00 [+0.82, +1.17] dB in the adult at top contact (Figure 6a, c), which brings Neuromag closer than its measured position (+1.27 dB there, area-weighted; Table 1). Every smaller head's point estimate of Δ is positive (Figure 6b): +0.44 to +1.07 dB in the scaled adults and infant templates, and smallest in the school-aged children (provisional examples; Section 2.2), +0.30 [+0.11, +0.38], +0.41 [+0.28, +0.54] and +0.17 [−0.07, +0.34] dB in A, B and C, child C's interval spanning zero.

Placement, which the parcel intervals exclude, is the larger uncertainty: over the source-blind placements of the fixed helmet Δ spans +0.20 to +1.12 dB in the school-age scaled adult and +0.06 to +0.37, +0.19 to +0.63 and −0.10 to +0.70 dB in children A, B and C, positive at every placement in every head except child C (Figure 7c), though crossed bands include zero in every child (S1, section E).

3.5 Helmet fit versus head size

Is the smaller heads' larger advantage a matter of head size, as the sphere suggests, or of the fixed helmet's fit? The helmet fitted at the adult's gap is the main control (Figure 7). In each scaled adult and template, the fitted helmet restores to Neuromag what it loses to the fixed helmet's gap and placement; this within-head contrast (the median over the head's targets of the paired difference; Figure 7d) is +0.17 to +0.76 dB at the adult's gap and +0.27 to +0.91 dB at its top-contact gap. Of the extra they show in the fixed helmet (+0.44 to +1.07 dB; Section 3.4), these heads keep little. Fitted at the adult's top-contact gap, they keep +0.10 to +0.23 dB against the adult at top contact (4 of 5 intervals above zero). Fitted at the adult's gap, Δ against the adult fitted the same way is +0.02 to +0.15 dB (point estimates, the highest the 18-month template's, which the MRI check classes as misregistered; interval bounds −0.09 to +0.33 dB, every template interval spanning zero and only the school-age scaled adult's just above zero). With the room field projected out, every smaller head's point estimate at the adult's gap is below the adult's (−0.67 to −0.05 dB; three of the eight intervals spanning zero). Measured against the adult placed by the same rule in both helmets, the reduction is the interaction, the extra that the fixed helmet gives a smaller head beyond what it gives the adult: +0.40 to +1.10 dB in the scaled adults and templates at the adult's gap, every interval above zero (per head in Table S6; +0.40 to +1.11 dB at its top-contact gap). The interaction exceeds the heads' own within-head contrasts because the adult's own contrast is negative, −0.23 dB: the adult's fitted helmet is its laterally centred pose, looser than top contact (at its top-contact gap, −0.11 dB, the effect of the construction itself). The templates' Δ in the fitted helmet may also be too low because of their scalp convention (Section 2.2).

In the children, provisional examples (Section 2.2), Δ at the adult's gap is negative in A and B (−0.30 [−0.48, −0.22] and −0.39 [−0.54, −0.17] dB), but fitted at the adult's top-contact gap and set against the adult at top contact it is not established (−0.11 [−0.40, +0.06] and −0.22 [−0.35, +0.01] dB; S1, section E); their within-head contrasts are +0.29 and +0.42 dB. In child C, whose two gaps nearly coincide (Table 2), Δ is +0.01 [−0.23, +0.18] dB, and its within-head contrast, −0.12 [−0.20, −0.02] dB, reflects the construction itself (worth −0.11 [−0.15, −0.08] dB in the adult at an equal median gap).

None of these quantities apportions the effects exactly, because the fitted helmet changes the gap distribution, the placement and the sensors' coverage together. In the fitted helmet and at equal OPM site count, Δ is +0.16 to +0.49 dB (S1, section E), and in the scaled adults and templates their fewer sites take about that much back; in children A and B no such gain is established (+0.16 [−0.12, +0.23] and +0.16 [−0.10, +0.41] dB). In this model, then, fitting this helmet to the adult's median gap removes most of the extra advantage that the scaled adults and templates show in the fixed adult helmet; it does not isolate the median gap as a cause.

3.6 Simulated interictal spikes

Does the detectability difference change which simulated spikes are detected, and how well they are localized?

Confirmatory run. The endpoint declared before the run (Section 2.5) passes in all nine anatomies (Table 3; Figure 8a). The S50 ratio Neuromag/OPM is 1.27 to 1.47, every interval above 1, and the Holm-adjusted p over the 36 newly drawn locations per anatomy is <0.0001 to 0.010. The nine anatomies are not independent replications (Section 2.2). The ratios are of the order of the adult's detectability ratio at these depths (1.36 to 1.60; Figure 4a), below it by point estimate in the 24-month template, child A, child B and child C (Table 3); in the children the OPM's lead at 10–30 mm is below the adult's by point estimate in 11 of 12 depth strata (S1, section E). All nine pass in each of the five noise replicates (ratio SD 2.9% to 6.4%; pooled 1.22 to 1.44). The oracle detector gives 1.27 to 1.62 and passes everywhere (Figure 8b).

Checks. On separate null data that no threshold saw, the frozen thresholds give 0.70 to 2.25 false events per minute for the two systems, rate-matched thresholds 0.70 to 1.60, with ratios of 1.27 to 1.50, all nine passing (the systems' rates still differ within anatomies; S1, section G). The mismatched detector (Section 2.5) changes the ratio by 0.91 to 1.11 (every interval spanning 1); the test passes in all nine anatomies (Figure 8c) and in 8 of 9 with rate-matched thresholds (child B, Holm-adjusted p = 0.072). The site-matched array's lower S50 passes in 5 of 9 anatomies, in none of the children (Table 3), its ratios 0.99 to 1.30, point estimates above 1 in 8 of 9, in line with its shallow lead (Section 3.3).

The exploratory run (18 of its 72 locations per anatomy in this band; endpoint chosen after the analyses) had pointed the same way (S50 ratios 1.18 to 1.65); its deeper bands and discordant events remain exploratory (S1, section G). Both runs kept the fixed helmet and the primary noise model: they do not test whether the gap drives a detection benefit in smaller heads, and spikes were not simulated under scenario D, under which the dense array's detectability ratio at these depths stays at 1.20 to 1.35 in the adult (S1, section C).

4. Discussion

4.1 Answers to the four questions

Q1. Across the conditions tested, the OPM noise floor or the array can reverse which system leads, and the comparator and the metric decide which reported number shows it. With sensor plus brain noise the dense array leads at every depth; the site-matched array has an equal-SNR depth, and so, by bin medians, does the dense array after the projection (an interval below 1 only at 55–60 mm) or with Neuromag's measured noise (scenario D, point estimates; Section 3.3). The dense lead survives the omissions tested at 15 fT/√Hz but is the size of the model's error for Neuromag: until an OPM covariance is measured, the answer is a conditional prediction.

Q2. Under the modelled noise the dense lead is largest on the frontal and central convexity and smallest in cingulate cortex, and the site-matched array trails on ventral and polar surfaces, including mesial temporal cortex; with Neuromag's measured noise (scenario D; point estimates) the frontal lead falls to about 1, the cingulate and parietal lobes fall below 1 and the temporal lobe leads (Section 3.3).

Q3. Fitting the helmet to the adult's median gap substantially reduces the extra advantage the smaller heads show in the fixed adult helmet: the interaction is +0.40 to +1.10 dB in the scaled adults and templates, every interval above zero. In the fixed helmet every smaller head's point estimate exceeds the adult's (child C's interval and placement band span zero); fitted at the adult's gap, Δ against the adult fitted the same way is +0.02 to +0.03 dB in the scaled adults and +0.04 to +0.15 dB in the templates, a residual that depends on the adult's reference placement (+0.10 to +0.23 dB when the heads are fitted at the adult's top-contact gap and set against the adult at top contact) and, for the templates, on their scalp convention. The construction changes gap, placement and coverage together, so it does not isolate the median gap as the cause. At equal OPM site count Δ is +0.16 to +0.49 dB (not established in children A and B), the quantity comparable to the sensor-noise-only result of Zahran et al. (2022), and in the scaled adults and templates their fewer OPM sites take it back; these controls do not show what produces either effect. The children's values, two of them below the adult fitted the same way but not established in that top-contact comparison, are provisional (Section 2.2).

Q4. For superficial spikes and the dense array, yes: in the confirmatory run it detected simulated spikes 10–20 mm deep at 21% to 32% lower strength than Neuromag in all nine anatomies (fixed adult helmet; idealized detector, modelled noise), and the ranking survived the declared detector mismatch; no (exploratory) localization difference is established.

4.2 The clinical literature: compatibility, not reconciliation

The clinical reports and the model are compatible, but the model does not identify which factor operated in any study (Table 4). Higher or similar OPM SNR against SQUID magnetometers (Feys et al., 2022; mixed in Feys et al., 2025) corresponds to peak-channel SNR against the magnetometers, which favours the OPM in every head (S1, section F), and equal or lower SNR against planar gradiometers or the nearest channels of few or noisier sensors (Hillebrand et al., 2023; Schwartz et al., 2025) to peak-channel SNR against the gradiometers or the best channel. The larger gains reported in school-aged children in an adult helmet (Feys et al., 2022) are compatible with the fixed adult helmet's fit (gap and placement together; Sections 3.4 and 3.5). The weaker OPM results for temporal and mesial temporal sources (Feys et al., 2024, 2025; Shen et al., 2026) have two analogues, deep sources being weaker for every system and the site-matched array trailing on ventral and polar surfaces; the second is fragile, because the model overstates Neuromag's detectability in the temporal lobe (measured over modelled 0.89; S1, section C). With a channel-averaged metric the site-matched array has a small but established lead (1.05 [1.04, 1.06]) where Matsubara et al. (2026) found no region with a higher OPM SNR (with brain noise only, in patients' anatomies), and a cohort's report of no significant difference in detection (Ren et al., 2025; abstract only) cannot adjudicate the model's predictions, whose endpoints differ from it (whole-cortex detectability; simulated superficial-spike detection, Section 3.6).

By whole-cortex detectability against Neuromag's 306 channels with the oracle covariance, a single-axis array like the site-matched one (98 sites, about the channel count of the larger clinical arrays) shows no established advantage at 15 fT/√Hz and a SQUID advantage when noisier (Section 3.1). Its small established advantages depend on the comparator, the metric, the covariance estimate or depth (within about 30 mm of the scalp, including simulated superficial spikes in 5 of 9 anatomies; Sections 3.1, 3.3 and 3.6; Table 4). Clinical arrays reach their channel counts with dual- or triaxial sensors at fewer sites; the triaxial control at the site-matched sites gives 1.12, showing what extra axes at the site-matched sites can recover; whether an array of about 64 dual-axis sites lies above or below the site-matched array, the model does not determine. The modest whole-cortex advantage belongs to an array with more sites than any used in epilepsy so far, at a white noise within the clinical range.

4.3 Limitations

No measurement validates the OPM covariance: the OPM sensor noise is declared and white, and Neuromag's measured noise alone favours Neuromag by as much as the dense array's whole lead (Section 3.2). A measured OPM noise covariance in the same band would decide Q1; public OPM recordings could give a first check. A single projection stands in for the preprocessing of real systems (Taulu & Simola, 2006; Tierney et al., 2021). One adult is the reference (its D spans 0.46 dB over 12 source-blind placements), the pediatric claims rest on two copies of it, three templates and three children of one dataset, and the parcel intervals exclude between-subject variability. The children's skull is modelled, their fiducials are transferred and unchecked on their MRIs, and the MRI check cannot settle whether their white surface is right within a few millimetres of the scalp; their negative Δ at the adult's gap may rest on that near-scalp cortex, which is why they are treated as provisional examples. The templates' scalps lie 1.3 to 1.6 mm outside their MRI head boundaries, the adult's 1.3 mm inside, so the templates' Δ may carry a difference of conventions of 2.6 to 3.0 mm, which could make it too low (for illustration only, a 3-mm OPM gap lowers the adult's dense ratio from 1.14 to 1.09; Section 3.1); the within-head contrast compares two helmets on one scalp and the interaction is a difference of two such contrasts, so both are less exposed to it. The 18-month template's cortex may also be displaced by up to about 2.3 mm relative to its T1, or its average image may have weak contrast (S1, section D); the effect of such an error was not estimated. The fitted-helmet result does not rest on the templates: in the two scaled adults, which keep the adult's anatomy, the fixed-helmet extra of +0.44 and +1.07 dB falls to +0.13 and +0.11 dB against the adult at top contact (the helmet fitted at its top-contact gap), and they give the lowest and the highest interaction of the scaled adults and templates (+0.40 and +1.10 dB; templates +0.54 to +0.70 dB). The smaller heads keep the adult's background variance per unit cortical area; their fixed-helmet gain stays positive when it is multiplied by 0.5 or 2 (S4). All sources lie on the cortical surface, so hippocampus and amygdala are absent. The spike simulations are idealized: the detector's templates, candidate patterns and whitening covariance come from the simulation's own stationary model, the null data hold no physiological transients, and the ranking was tested against one declared mismatch under the primary noise model only. Head motion was bounded and is not part of the spike simulations (S1, section G).

4.4 Conclusions

Across the conditions tested, the OPM noise floor or the array can reverse which system leads, while source depth changes the size of the difference, and the comparator and the metric decide whether a reported SNR shows it. The modest lead of a dense array is a conditional prediction until an OPM noise covariance is measured; a single-axis array at Neuromag's 98 sites, about the channel count of the larger clinical arrays, shows no established whole-cortex advantage with the oracle covariance at the primary noise level (a small one, 1.03 [1.01, 1.05], with a covariance estimated from 4,680 samples) and a SQUID advantage when noisier. The dense lead is smallest in cingulate cortex and the site-matched array trails on ventral and polar surfaces (exploratory rankings). Fitting the helmet to the adult's median gap removes most of the extra advantage that the scaled adults and templates show in the fixed adult helmet (an interaction of +0.40 to +1.10 dB); how much remains depends on the adult's reference placement, and the children are provisional examples. A confirmatory run (idealized detector, modelled noise, fixed adult helmet) found a dense array detecting simulated superficial spikes at a lower strength than Neuromag in all nine anatomies (not independent replications); no (exploratory) localization difference is established. Clinical reports of higher OPM SNR are compatible with these conditions, which the model cannot tell apart in any study.
