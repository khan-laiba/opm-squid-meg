\begin{abstract}
Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) can be placed on the scalp, where the magnetic field of cortical sources is stronger than at the superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) of conventional magnetoencephalography (MEG). For interictal epileptiform discharges, however, OPMs have shown a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) than SQUIDs in some comparisons but not in others. A spherical-head analysis with point sensors at fixed standoffs and an assumed OPM-to-SQUID noise ratio predicted the largest on-scalp benefit for superficial sources and small heads. Using realistic head models, we examined how the relative SNR depends on noise, array design and helmet fit. We compared the matched-filter detectability of cortical sources under sensor, brain and environmental noise between the 306-channel Neuromag SQUID system and two single-axis OPM arrays, one dense (208 sensors) and one at the SQUID sites (98 sensors). An adult head, two downscaled copies and two infant templates were each placed in the adult helmet and in a helmet fitted at the adult's median sensor-to-scalp distance. In the adult, brain noise, which dominated in both systems, reversed the ranking obtained under sensor noise alone. At an OPM noise of 15~fT/√Hz, the dense array had the higher detectability at almost all cortical sources (median ratio 1.14, 95\,\% parcel-bootstrap interval [1.12, 1.17]), whereas OPMs at the SQUID sites showed no clear difference. The OPM noise level and array design determined which system had the higher detectability; the choice of SQUID channels and SNR measure determined which system a sensor-level SNR favored. The dense-array advantage, however, was as large as the noise model's underestimate of SQUID detectability: with measured SQUID noise and modeled OPM noise, the ratio fell to 1.03 [0.99, 1.08]. In the adult helmet, the smaller heads had greater sensor-to-scalp distances and a larger dense-array advantage than the adult (by +0.44 to +1.07~dB). In the fitted helmet, with fewer dense-array sites on each smaller head, almost none of this excess remained (+0.02 to +0.05~dB). Under the modeled noise, the dense array detected simulated superficial spikes at a 22–32\,\% lower dipole strength than the SQUID system, with idealized detectors at a nominal false-event rate. Overall, the results suggest that noise, array design and helmet fit, rather than sensor proximity alone, determine whether OPMs provide a higher SNR than SQUIDs, which is of interest when comparing OPM and SQUID recordings in pediatric and epilepsy studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In conventional magnetoencephalography (MEG), the sensors are superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) housed in a rigid cryogenic helmet. Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs), in contrast, measure the magnetic field of the brain at room temperature and can therefore be worn on the scalp \citep{boto2017,iivanainen2017}. Because the SQUID helmet is built for adults, a child's head is separated from its sensors by a wide and uneven gap, whereas an on-scalp array can fit any head \citep{feysdetiege2024,hill2019}. In clinical recordings in epilepsy, interictal discharges are larger with OPMs than with cryogenic MEG \citep{feys2022,ren2025}. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of these discharges, however, was higher with OPMs in some comparisons \citep{feys2022,feys2025,ren2025} and equal or slightly lower in others \citep{hillebrand2023,schwartz2025}. The weakest OPM results concern temporal and mesial temporal sources \citep{feys2024,feys2025,shen2026}.

One might expect on-scalp sensors to detect every source better, simply because they are closer to it. However, an OPM is a noisier sensor than a SQUID magnetometer, so a larger field does not by itself yield a higher SNR. A spherical-head analysis made this trade-off explicit, with a point magnetometer on the scalp for the OPM and one 18~mm above it for the SQUID \citep{jas2026}. At intermediate ratios of OPM to SQUID noise, the OPM had the higher single-channel SNR only for sources shallower than an equal-SNR depth, i.e., the depth at which the two sensors have the same SNR. In an adult head, this depth was about 28~mm when the OPM noise was three times the SQUID noise. Because the SQUID sensors were placed at the same distance from the scalp for every head size, a larger proportion of a small brain is shallower than the equal-SNR depth. The analysis therefore predicts a larger OPM benefit in children.

A realistic comparison adds three elements to this picture: sensor, brain and room noise as each array records it, arrays of finite size on a real scalp, and a real SQUID helmet around a smaller head. Some of these elements have been added in earlier simulations. When only sensor noise was modeled, an array of helium-4 OPMs improved relative to SQUID magnetometers as the head shrank, both in a standard adult placement and in a helmet fitted at a constant gap \citep{zahran2022}. When brain noise was added in one adult, simulated spikes were visible on the scalp from smaller cortical patches with on-scalp than with conventional MEG \citep{westin2023}. In contrast, when brain noise but no sensor noise was modeled in epilepsy patients, OPMs placed at the SQUID sites did not raise the channel-averaged SNR in any region. The OPM--SQUID difference nevertheless followed the ratio of sensor distances, which tracked head circumference \citep{matsubara2026}.

None of these studies compared head size and helmet fit with brain noise included, and the only helmet fitted at a constant gap \citep{zahran2022} matched the adult's closest scalp-to-sensor distance, not the adult's median gap. Whether the larger OPM advantage expected in children reflects the small head itself or the poor fit of the adult helmet has therefore not been tested. In addition, none of these studies checked its noise model against a measured covariance. Nor is it clear how far a modeled comparison can be trusted while the OPM noise has not been measured.

In the present study, we compared the 306-channel Neuromag system with two OPM arrays in a realistic adult head and in smaller heads: a dense array packed over the scalp, and a site-matched array with one OPM at each Neuromag site. As the measure, we used the detectability of each cortical source by a matched filter that knows the field pattern of the source and the covariance of the sensor, brain and room noise. First, we asked what determines the comparison in the adult, and we checked the noise model against Neuromag's measured noise. Second, we placed smaller heads in the adult helmet and in a helmet fitted to each head at the adult's gap, to separate head size from helmet fit. Third, we asked whether the detectability difference carries over to the detection of simulated interictal spikes by idealized detectors. We also examined regional differences and spike localization as exploratory questions. In this model, proximity alone did not decide the comparison. The array, the OPM noise floor and the fit of the SQUID helmet, which sets the distance of the SQUIDs from a smaller head, determined whether and by how much the on-scalp array had the higher detectability. This result bears on how OPM and SQUID recordings are compared in children and in epilepsy.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Study design and estimands}\label{sec:design}

To compare arrays without committing to a particular detector, we used the known-topography detectability of each source. The known-topography detectability is the output SNR of the matched filter that knows both the field pattern of the source and the covariance of the noise,
\begin{equation}
d = \sqrt{\symbf{s}^{\top}\symbf{C}^{-1}\symbf{s}}.
\label{eq:d}
\end{equation}
Here, $\symbf{s}$ is the noise-free field of a 10-nAm cortical dipole at the channels of the array. The matrix $\symbf{C}$ is the noise covariance of the array (Section~\ref{sec:noise}), taken as known, i.e., the true covariance of the modeled noise (the oracle covariance). Two arrays were then compared target by target, by the ratio $d_{\mathrm{OPM}}/d_{\mathrm{Neuromag}}$ or, in decibels, by
\begin{equation}
D = 20\log_{10}\frac{d_{\mathrm{OPM}}}{d_{\mathrm{Neuromag}}},
\label{eq:D}
\end{equation}
which is positive when the OPM array has the higher detectability.

Each comparison was then summarized over the cortex. For the adult, we took the unweighted median of the target-wise ratios over all 7,661 targets, including the 558 vertices on the medial wall. We set this convention for the adult before the smaller heads were added, and we retained it rather than change it after the results were known. For the dense array, excluding these vertices would change the median ratio to 1.15, and the area-weighted convention of the smaller heads would give 1.16 (+1.27~dB). Because the smaller heads differ in cortical area, we summarized each of them by the area-weighted median of $D$ over its cortical targets, with the medial wall excluded.

To compare a smaller head with the adult, we placed both in the same helmet and pose (Section~\ref{sec:arrays}) and computed the change from the adult, $\Delta$, from paired differences rather than as the difference of their medians. How the differences were paired depended on the anatomy. For the scaled adults, which are downscaled copies of the adult and share its vertices, $\Delta$ was computed as the median of $D_{\mathrm{head}}(v) - D_{\mathrm{adult}}(v)$ over common vertices, weighted by the target areas of the smaller head. For the other heads, which have their own cortical surfaces and therefore do not share the adult's vertices, the area-weighted median of $D$ was first taken within every parcel of the Desikan--Killiany atlas that contains at least 10 targets in both heads. In the 24- and 12-month templates, 66 and 65 parcels, respectively, met this criterion. The change from the adult was then computed as the median of the parcel differences, weighted by the adult's parcel areas.

\subsection{Head models}\label{sec:heads}

We used the MNE sample subject as the adult head \citep{gramfort2013}, with its FreeSurfer surfaces and atlas labels \citep{desikan2006,fischl2012}. The targets were the vertices of an MNE-Python oct-6 source space on the white-matter surface, each modeled as a 10-nAm dipole normal to the cortex. Only vertices at least 4~mm inside the inner skull were retained. The depth of a target was defined as its distance to the nearest point of the MRI scalp. Forward fields were computed with a three-layer boundary-element model (BEM; \citealp{hamalainen1989}) with conductivities of 0.3, 0.006, 0.3~S/m, whose outer surface was the MRI scalp. The BEM was solved by linear collocation, with 5,120 triangles per skull surface and the scalp refined to 20,480 triangles. We checked this BEM in two ways. First, compared with the field of an exact three-shell sphere, the field of this BEM had a 95th-percentile error of 0.074\,\% at 2~mm and 0.038\,\% at 15~mm outside the outer surface. Second, on a subset of targets, we computed the ratio of the dense array with a scalp of 5,120 triangles and with the refined scalp of 20,480 triangles. The refinement changed this ratio from 1.149 to 1.141.

Of the eight smaller heads (Table~\ref{tab:anatomies}), four form the basis of the main analysis, and we refer to them as the principal smaller heads. Two of them were made by scaling the adult, one to school-age size and the other to the head circumference of the 24-month template. Because these scaled adults preserve the adult anatomy exactly, they are the main test of head size and helmet fit. The other two are the 24- and 12-month infant templates, i.e., averages of many MRIs \citep{oreilly2021,richards2016}, which add infant head shapes to the analysis, subject to the caveat about their scalp convention given below. None of the anatomies, however, is an independent replication, because all of them share the noise calibration, the adult's conductivities and the processing.

The remaining four heads are reported separately in Section~S10. The 18-month template was classed as misregistered by an MRI check, because its white surface fitted its T1-weighted image best after a 2.3-mm shift. In the other two templates, the corresponding shift was 0.5~mm. The MRIs of three typically developing school-aged children, A, B and C (7.8–8.7 years), were taken from OpenNeuro dataset ds005234 \citep{fadeev2024,fadeev2025}. Their anatomy was prepared in three further steps (Section~S4). First, because in the dataset the inner skull of these children was a median of 0.8–2.5~mm below the scalp over the upper head, we displaced it inward, toward 8~mm below the scalp, without bringing it closer than 2~mm to the white-surface vertices. Second, we transferred their fiducials from the adult by a similarity transform fitted between the white surfaces. Third, we checked their surfaces against their T1-weighted MRIs (Fig.~S6). The surfaces were found to be registered to the images, but the check cannot verify the cortex within a few millimeters of the scalp. Because the 4-mm limit on targets also left out 94–100\,\% of the children's cortex within 8~mm of the scalp, their results do not describe a near-scalp cortex.

We made two further checks of the principal heads. First, the infant templates follow a different scalp convention from the adult's: their scalps are 1.3–1.6~mm outside their own MRI head boundaries, whereas the adult's scalp is 1.3~mm inside its boundary, a difference of 2.6–3.0~mm between the conventions (Section~S4.3). Second, replacing the three-layer BEM with a single-compartment BEM, which has no skull or scalp conductivity layer, changed the difference between the median $D$ of each principal head and that of the adult by −0.02 to +0.01~dB. This check tests the conductivity layers, not the position of the scalp or the inner skull.

\subsection{Sensor arrays and helmets}\label{sec:arrays}

We modeled the Neuromag system (306 channels; ``Neuromag'' below) with the sensor geometry of the sample recording and the T3 coil definitions of MNE-Python (types 3014 and 3024). Each gradiometer was integrated at 8 points and each magnetometer at 16. Neuromag's white noise was set to the typical value of the TRIUX datasheet, 3.5~fT/√Hz for the magnetometers and 3.6~fT/(cm~√Hz) for the gradiometers \citep{elekta_triux}. The Neuromag coils were 18~mm outward from the inner surface of the helmet (the Dewar spacing), and no signal-space separation was applied. We compared each OPM array with all 306 Neuromag channels and, as alternative comparators, with the 204 gradiometers or the 102 magnetometers alone. Besides detectability, we computed two sensor-level measures, the peak-channel SNR and the mean-power SNR \citep{goldenholz2009}, which are defined in Table~\ref{tab:estimands}.

Each OPM was modeled as a single-axis sensor whose sensing center was nominally 7~mm above the MRI scalp \citep{alem2023,osborne2018}, and sites whose sensor cell would otherwise have come within 1~mm of the head surface were displaced outward. In the adult, 26 of the 208 dense sites and 11 of the 98 site-matched sites were displaced in this way. The field of each OPM was averaged over a 10-mm cube with a 27-point Gauss--Legendre rule, a modeling choice that changes the peak field by at most 1.9\,\%. The sensitive axis was taken as the head-surface normal, averaged within 15~mm of the site. Had nearly radial axes been used instead, the ratio of the dense array with the room field projected out would have been 1.07 rather than 1.12 (Sections~\ref{sec:noise} and S3). We set the OPM white noise to 15~fT/√Hz in the primary analysis, flat over 1–40~Hz, and varied it over 7, 10, 15, 20 and 30~fT/√Hz.

Both OPM arrays were restricted to the same region of the scalp: the part above the plane through the preauricular points and a point 30~mm above the nasion, with no OPM within 20~mm of either preauricular point. The dense array was packed into this region at a minimum spacing of 17~mm, which gave 208 sites on the adult, more than any clinical OPM array in the studies of Table~S19. For the site-matched array, one OPM was placed at each Neuromag site whose projection onto the scalp fell inside the region, 98 of the 102 sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrays}). Clinical systems, in contrast, reach about 100--128 channels with dual- or triaxial sensors at fewer sites \citep{feys2025,shen2026}. We did not model such arrays. Neither single-axis array represents one, because both differ from clinical arrays in site count and sensing axes. To test the effect of extra axes, however, we added a triaxial array at the site-matched sites (294 channels).

In the fixed adult helmet, the adult's sensors and noise were used unchanged for every head (Fig.~\ref{fig:helmets}). For the primary placement in this helmet, each head was first posed with its head coordinate frame where the adult's had been recorded. It was then raised until the nearest magnetometer coil was 20~mm from the scalp, a best-fit assumption that favors Neuromag \citep{gaetz2008,tesan2010}. We call this placement top contact. To describe the effect of placement, we also used a family of 12 source-blind placements, i.e., placements chosen from the geometry alone (contacts, shifts and rotations; Section~S5). The OPM arrays, in contrast, were rebuilt on every smaller head: the dense array by the same coverage and spacing rules, and the site-matched array from the Neuromag sites of that head at top contact (Table~\ref{tab:anatomies}).

To separate head size from helmet fit, we also placed each head in a helmet fitted at the adult's gap. The helmet was fitted in two steps. First, each head was translated laterally from the recorded pose onto the midline of the helmet, without being raised to contact (the laterally centered pose). Second, the helmet was scaled about the head origin until the median magnetometer-to-scalp gap equaled that of the adult in the same pose, 29.6~mm. For comparison, the adult's gap is 29.8~mm at its measured head position and 28.4~mm at top contact. No coil was allowed within the 18-mm Dewar spacing of the scalp, a limit that was not reached in any principal head. The fitted helmet retains the adult's sensors and therefore does not model a pediatric SQUID system.

Our primary measure of helmet fit is the paired change, from the adult to a smaller head, in how much detectability Neuromag loses in the fixed helmet relative to the helmet fitted at the adult's gap. We call this measure the interaction. For each target, the contrast between the two helmets within one head is
\begin{equation}
F(v) = D_{\mathrm{fixed}}(v) - D_{\mathrm{fitted}}(v) = 20\log_{10}\frac{d_{\mathrm{Neuromag,\,fitted}}(v)}{d_{\mathrm{Neuromag,\,fixed}}(v)},
\label{eq:F}
\end{equation}
in which the detectability of the OPM cancels. Because the fitted helmet is built about the laterally centered pose rather than at contact, $F$ combines the change of helmet scale with this change of pose. For a smaller head, the interaction is obtained by applying the $\Delta$ estimator of Section~\ref{sec:design} to $F$ instead of $D$. For the scaled adults, it is therefore the median of $F_{\mathrm{head}}(v) - F_{\mathrm{adult}}(v)$ over common vertices, weighted by the target areas of the smaller head. For the templates, it is the median of the parcel differences, weighted by the adult's parcel areas. Because the interaction is a median of paired differences, it equals neither the difference of the two median contrasts nor $\Delta_{\mathrm{fixed}} - \Delta_{\mathrm{fitted}}$, and Table~S6 gives the non-additivity residual of each head. The interaction does not depend on the assumed OPM noise, because the detectability of the OPM array, which is the same in both helmets, cancels in $F$; it does, however, depend on the modeled Neuromag noise and on the anatomy.

We used three secondary controls of the construction (Section~S5). First, one helmet was fitted at the adult's top-contact gap, 28.4~mm. Second, another helmet was scaled with the head, which scales the gap as well. Third, to compare the heads at an equal OPM site count, the helmet fitted at the adult's gap was used unchanged, but the adult's dense array was subsampled to the number of sites of each smaller head.

\subsection{Noise model, validation and sensitivity analyses}\label{sec:noise}

All arrays were exposed to the same noise sources in one band of 1–40~Hz. The noise had three parts: white sensor noise, a cortical background of spontaneous brain activity, and the field of distant sources in the room (the room field). The noise covariance of each array, which the detectability measure takes as known (the oracle covariance), was computed in closed form as
\begin{equation}
\symbf{C} = \symbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{n}} + \sigma^{2}\,\symbf{L}\symbf{A}\symbf{L}^{\top} + \symbf{E}\symbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{e}}\symbf{E}^{\top}.
\label{eq:C}
\end{equation}
Here, $\symbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{n}}$ is the diagonal covariance of white sensor noise. The brain noise enters through the second term, in which $\symbf{L}$ is the lead-field matrix of 1,755 independent cortex-normal background dipoles on a 7-mm grid and $\symbf{A}$ is the diagonal matrix of their Voronoi-cell areas. The room field enters through the last term, in which $\symbf{E}$ contains the responses of the array to eight external-field terms (three uniform and five linear-gradient components) and $\symbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{e}}$ is the covariance of their coefficients. The background is a cortically constrained form of the random-dipole model of spontaneous activity \citep{demunck1992}. Each background dipole has a moment variance of $\sigma^{2}$ times its area ($\sigma^{2}$ = 0.0354~nAm$^{2}$ per mm$^{2}$), and, as for the targets, the cortex within 4~mm of the inner skull is omitted from the background.

We fitted two quantities to the sample dataset: the room-field covariance $\symbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{e}}$, to the empty-room recording of the dataset, and $\sigma^{2}$, which was set so that the modeled brain noise of the median good gradiometer matched the measured one, a root-mean-square value of 37.1~fT/cm. Everything else, apart from the datasheet sensor noise of both systems, is predicted rather than fitted. Because these predictions come from the same recording, however, the checks below test the model within that recording, not in another subject or session. We analyzed four noise conditions. The first three were sensor noise only; sensor plus brain noise, the primary condition; and sensor, brain and room noise. To represent interference suppression, the fourth condition was derived from the third by removing the room field from every array with one projection, which removes the array's responses to the room-field terms after weighting each channel by its inverse sensor-noise variance (Eq.~(S1)). We designated this condition, the room field projected out, as the secondary condition before any simulation.

To check the model, we compared its Neuromag covariance with the measured covariance and recomputed Neuromag's detectability with the measured one (Section~S3). For this check, the recording was band-passed to 1–40~Hz, and one bad channel was excluded. As the measured covariance, we used the empirical covariance, without regularization or projections, of the 320 pre-stimulus windows (−0.2 to 0~s; 38,400 samples) of the recording. The brain noise of the measured covariance was taken as its difference from the empty-room covariance (63,666 samples). Because a covariance estimated from finite data inflates detectability, we removed this inflation with surrogates. We generated 40 phase-randomized surrogates of the whole recording, which preserve its spectra and cross-spectra, estimated their covariance from the same samples, and divided the measured detectability of each target by the resulting inflation (median factor 1.033).

Because the OPM covariance cannot be measured here, we computed the implied OPM/Neuromag ratio under five assumptions about how each system's real noise departs from the model. In scenario A, both systems share the model's error, so the modeled ratio stands. Scenario B scales both cortical backgrounds by $k$ = 1.86, the factor that makes the modeled magnetometer brain noise match the measured one. The other three scenarios replace all or part of Neuromag's modeled noise with measured noise. In scenarios C and D, Neuromag takes its measured covariance; the OPM background is scaled by $k$ in scenario C, whereas the OPM retains its modeled covariance in scenario D. In scenario E, Neuromag takes its measured empty-room noise together with the modeled brain noise.

In each sensitivity analysis, we changed one element of the adult model and recalibrated $\sigma^{2}$. First, the cortex 2--4~mm from the inner skull, which we call the near-skull cortex, was added to the background, to the targets, or to both. Second, the OPM noise was made colored (1/$f$ plus white noise, with corners at 1, 3 and 10~Hz) under a matched filter that also whitens across frequency. Third, idealized cardiac and ocular sources were added. Finally, the joint runs combined these changes: the near-skull cortex was added to the background and the OPM noise was given a 10-Hz corner under the frequency-whitening detector, with and without the cardiac and ocular sources. Noise specific to OPMs, e.g., cross-talk between neighboring sensors and errors in their gain and sensitive axis, was not modeled, and the distance-independent low-frequency noise of a real OPM (Section~S3.2) was represented only by the colored-noise variants.

\subsection{Simulated interictal spikes}\label{sec:spikes}

The spike simulations were designed to ask whether the detectability difference carries over to the detection of transient events by an idealized detector under the modeled noise. They do not test the noise model. For these simulations, the noise model was run in the time domain, in segments shared by all arrays, and the spike was the spike-wave complex of \citet{hunold2016}, stretched to three morphologies. The data were analyzed with two matched-filter detectors, both whitened with a Ledoit--Wolf covariance \citep{ledoit2004} estimated from 10~min of null data rather than with the oracle covariance.

The first, which we call the oracle detector, knew the source and the time and tested at a per-trial false-positive probability of 0.001. The second, the practical detector, knew neither. Instead, it scanned candidate field patterns on a 10-mm grid that did not contain the true sources, and its thresholds for a nominal 1 false event per minute were fixed in advance (frozen) on independent null data. Note that both detectors are idealized, because they use the true forward model and the simulated waveform family. We interpolated S50, the dipole strength detected in 50\,\% of events, linearly in log strength between the tested strengths. In both spike runs, the smaller heads were placed at top contact in the fixed adult helmet, whereas the adult was placed at its measured head position. At that position, the adult's area-weighted $D$ is 0.28~dB higher than at top contact.

In the exploratory run, spikes were placed at 72 locations per anatomy, stratified by orientation and by depth, with 18 locations in each of four depth bands. The bands ranged from 10–20 to 45–70~mm. At each location, 18 focal events of 10–320~nAm and patch events from cortical patches of 10-mm radius were simulated, each event in its own noise realization and identical for every array. Every comparison was made within one anatomy, and the anatomies are not ranked against one another. In addition, we localized the simulated spikes at 24 locations by equivalent-current-dipole fits and by dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM; \citealp{dale2000}) at the true peak sample, through a single-compartment BEM with a coregistration error of 2~mm and 2°. Every event, detected or not, was used in the paired comparisons of localization error, and a joint measure also required detection (Section~S7).

We then pre-specified a second run, whose endpoint compared the dense OPM array with Neuromag under the practical detector at a nominal 1 false event per minute, for focal spikes 10–20~mm below the scalp in every anatomy. For this run, we drew 36 new locations per anatomy and five noise replicates per event, the first of which carried the endpoint, and used separate null data for threshold fitting and for rate evaluation. The threshold, set on 20~min of calibration null data, rests on about 20 false events, which corresponds to a Poisson uncertainty of about 22\,\%. The oracle detector's threshold, in contrast, does not depend on that calibration.

The declared statistic was a two-sided sign-flip test on the per-location differences in detection counts, Holm-adjusted over the anatomies \citep{holm1979} at $\alpha$ = 0.05. The paired S50 ratio $r$ served as the effect size; a value of $r$ means that the OPM array detects a spike at a strength $100\,(1 - 1/r)$\,\% lower than Neuromag. Whereas the declared test estimated p by Monte Carlo when more than 20 location differences were nonzero, we report exact p values over all sign patterns, which give the same decisions in all 25 families of Holm-adjusted tests (Section~S7). As secondary analyses, we declared a mismatched detector (off-template morphologies, candidate patterns from a single-compartment BEM and a shared coregistration error of 2~mm and 2°) and analyzed the site-matched array, the oracle detector and thresholds re-fitted on held-out null data.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}

To preserve the dependence between neighboring targets within a parcel, intervals over targets were computed as 95\,\% percentile bootstrap intervals that resample atlas parcels as units, i.e., the adult's 70 labels or the parcels of each smaller head. We used 1,000 resamples for the primary comparisons, the noise-model sensitivity analyses and the measured-covariance check, and 200 for the other sensitivity analyses of the adult and for some secondary constructions of the smaller heads (Table~S16). For $\Delta$ and the interaction, which are medians of paired differences between a smaller head and the adult, the bootstrap resamples the parcels together with their paired differences. In the spike study, locations were the units of resampling and testing.

The parcel intervals describe how sensitive a whole-cortex median is to the parcels it pools, within one anatomy and one noise model. They are neither population confidence intervals nor a measure of the spread of effects over the cortex, and they exclude the larger uncertainties of model error, head placement and between-subject variation. We therefore report whether an interval includes 1 (or 0~dB) as a description, not as a test. The main estimands are listed in Table~\ref{tab:estimands}, and all of them in Table~S2.

Several choices were made after results were known. The exploratory spike endpoint, which was chosen from 24 comparisons per OPM array, the primary helmet placement and the regions of interest were all named after the whole-cortex results were known. In addition, the helmet fitted at the adult's gap, the check of the noise model against the measured covariance with its five scenarios, and the sensitivity analyses were added later, without changing the model. The model itself, however, was revised while results were known (Section~S8.2). The pre-specified run was declared in a configuration file committed to the public code repository the day before the run, which used the same code and configuration. Only the history of the repository attests this; the run was not registered externally. The number of locations in this run, double the exploratory 18 per band, was chosen after two pilot runs in child A. The outputs of these pilot runs were not retained, and their seeds were not reused.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Determinants of the adult comparison}\label{sec:res-adult}

Brain noise reversed the comparison between the dense OPM array and Neuromag (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}; Table~\ref{tab:adult}). When only sensor noise was modeled, Neuromag had a higher detectability than either OPM array (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}), but when brain noise was added, the dense array had the higher detectability at 7,657 of the 7,661 cortical targets. Over all targets, its median detectability ratio to Neuromag was 1.14 [1.12, 1.17]. In contrast, the site-matched array did not differ clearly from Neuromag (1.01 [0.99, 1.03]). The same pattern was observed when the room field was projected out of every array, although the ratio of the site-matched array was then slightly below parity.

Brain noise dominated the noise of both systems (Section~S3). When the room field was included, brain noise accounted for 92\,\% of the in-band variance of an OPM channel in the dense array and for 74\,\% of that of a Neuromag magnetometer. The room field accounted for most of the remaining magnetometer variance (25\,\%). As a result, brain noise raised the noise of a Neuromag magnetometer about 9.3-fold above its sensor floor, but that of an OPM only about 5.7-fold, so that the higher sensor noise of the OPM mattered less once brain noise was present.

The ratio also depended on which Neuromag channels served as the comparator and on the SNR measure (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}; Table~\ref{tab:adult}). Against the gradiometers alone, the dense array had a larger advantage than against the magnetometers alone (Table~\ref{tab:adult}). Peak-channel SNR is the closest analog of the sensor-level SNRs reported in clinical studies (Section~S1). By this measure, the dense array had the higher SNR when compared with the magnetometers, whereas Neuromag had the higher SNR when its best channel of all 306 was used. With mean-power SNR, both OPM arrays showed only small advantages (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}). Which system a reported number favors thus depends on the Neuromag channels and the SNR measure that it uses.

Of the model variants, the white-noise floor of the OPMs had the largest effect on the ratio (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}D; Table~\ref{tab:adult}). As the OPM white noise was raised over the range tested, the ratio of the dense array decreased to parity, whereas that of the site-matched array fell below it (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}D). For the dense array, the break-even OPM noise, i.e., the OPM white noise at which the ratio equals 1 under the modeled noise, was 31.5 [28.5, 34.9]~fT/√Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}D, diamonds). The site-matched array broke even at 16.7 [14.0, 19.4]~fT/√Hz, an interval that includes the primary OPM noise level. With the room field projected out, the break-even level of the dense array fell to 26.0 [22.3, 29.5]~fT/√Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}D, open diamonds). That of the site-matched array fell to 8.9 [5.6, 12.4]~fT/√Hz. Note that all of these levels assume the modeled Neuromag noise, which the next section compares with the measured noise.

The other model variants changed the ratio of the dense array less over the ranges tested (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}; Table~\ref{tab:adult}). An additional OPM-to-scalp gap reduced the ratio but did not reverse it. Combining an OPM noise of 30~fT/√Hz with an additional 3-mm gap, however, did reverse it (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}). With both changes, the ratio was 0.97 [0.96, 0.98]. A triaxial array at the site-matched sites recovered most of the advantage of the dense array, and it still recovered part of that advantage when its tangential axes were twice as noisy (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}, triangles). The ratio changed little when the covariance was estimated from a finite number of simulated samples, as many as a minute of data would contain, or when a 3--70-Hz band was used instead of the primary band. Thus, apart from brain noise, the array and the OPM noise floor determined which system had the higher detectability in the adult, whereas the comparator and the SNR measure determined which system a reported SNR favored.

\subsection{The noise model against measured Neuromag noise}\label{sec:res-noise}

The noise model reproduced some properties of Neuromag's measured noise and missed others (Figs.~S2 and S3). For the magnetometers, the model predicted 0.73 of the measured brain-noise amplitude, a quantity not used in its calibration, and 0.89 with the field of the heart removed. When the brain-noise scale was calibrated on a random half of the sites, the predicted gradiometer level of the other half agreed with the measurement nearly by construction (1.00; Fig.~S3B). Over 1,000 random splits, the 95\,\% range of this model-to-measured ratio was [0.93, 1.09]. However, the modeled brain noise was too flat from inferior to superior sites. When the model was calibrated on the inferior half of the sites, it predicted 1.34 times the measured gradiometer amplitude of the superior half, and when it was calibrated on the superior half, it predicted 0.75 times that of the inferior half. In addition, the model predicted the per-channel variances poorly, and its magnetometer correlations decreased with distance faster than the measured correlations did. Thus, the measured covariance differed from the modeled one in structure as well as in level.

The measured brain noise also contained variance that the model lacks. This excess, the positive part of the difference between the measured and modeled magnetometer brain-noise covariances, summed to 1.10 times the summed variance of the modeled brain noise, whereas sampling error alone would produce an excess of 0.02–0.05 times that variance. It was concentrated in a few far-field components (Section~S3). Neuromag's detectability was nevertheless higher with its measured covariance than with the modeled one (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}A). The median ratio of the two detectabilities was 1.14 [1.08, 1.20], a factor as large as the whole advantage of the dense array, and it varied by lobe, from 0.89 in the temporal lobe to 1.40 in the cingulate. This may appear paradoxical, but it follows from how detectability weighs the noise. Because detectability depends only on the noise along the field pattern of each source after whitening, the measured noise must be lower than the model along the cortical field patterns, with its excess in directions that the whitening down-weights.

What the measured Neuromag noise implies for the OPM depends on the real OPM noise, which no measurement here constrains (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}B; Table~\ref{tab:adult}). The ratios of both arrays were nearly unchanged when the two systems shared the model's error (scenario A), when both cortical backgrounds were scaled to match the measured magnetometer brain noise (scenario B), or when Neuromag was given its measured empty-room noise and modeled brain noise (scenario E). In contrast, scenario C, in which Neuromag was given its measured covariance and the OPM background was scaled as in scenario B, was the least favorable to the OPM and lowered the ratios of both arrays below parity. When Neuromag was given its measured covariance and the OPM noise remained as modeled (scenario D), the ratio of the dense array decreased to 1.03 [0.99, 1.08], an interval that includes 1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}B). The ratio of the site-matched array fell to 0.89 [0.86, 0.92], below parity. Under scenario D, neither OPM array therefore had a clear advantage over Neuromag at the primary OPM noise level.

We did not compute break-even levels under scenario D, but their direction follows from the ratios above. Because Neuromag's measured detectability does not change with the OPM noise, the ratio under this scenario, like the modeled ratio, falls as the OPM noise rises. With a ratio of 1.03 at 15~fT/√Hz, the dense array would therefore break even above that level at the point estimate, although the interval of its ratio includes 1. With a ratio of 0.89, the site-matched array would break even below it, if at all within the tested range. How far either level would move from its modeled value cannot be inferred from the medians alone, because the correction for the measured noise varies across targets.

The model omissions that we tested changed the advantage of the dense array little at the primary OPM noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}C; Table~\ref{tab:adult}). The ratio was unchanged when the near-skull cortex was added as background or as targets (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}C). Colored OPM noise with the highest corner frequency tested, 10~Hz, reduced the ratio slightly, both under the primary detector and under one that also whitens across frequency. Similarly, adding cardiac and ocular sources changed the ratio by only −1.6\,\% to +1.6\,\%.

The joint runs combined the near-skull background with a 10-Hz corner of the OPM noise under the frequency-whitening detector (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}D, dashed). With white OPM noise at 15~fT/√Hz, this detector gave the dense array a ratio of 1.13 [1.10, 1.15]. The joint run reduced it to 1.08 [1.06, 1.11] (Table~\ref{tab:adult}), whereas raising the white OPM noise alone to 20~fT/√Hz reduced it more, to 1.06 [1.03, 1.08]. With both changes together, the ratio was close to parity at 20~fT/√Hz (1.016 [0.997, 1.035], and 1.02 [1.00, 1.05] with the cardiac and ocular sources added) and below it at 30~fT/√Hz (0.93; Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}D). Under the same detector, the omitted noise sources thus lowered the ratio less than a rise of the OPM noise from 15 to 20~fT/√Hz did.

\subsection{Depth and brain regions}\label{sec:res-depth}

The advantage of the dense array was largest near the scalp and decreased with depth (Fig.~\ref{fig:depth}A). Its ratio decreased from 1.60 [1.55, 1.65] in the 10–15~mm depth bin to 1.13 [1.12, 1.14] in the 25–30~mm bin and then changed little, remaining at 1.05–1.07 from the 35–40 to the 55–60~mm bin. Even the deepest targets thus kept a small advantage, and every interval in these bins excluded parity. The site-matched array, in contrast, had an equal-SNR depth, as in the spherical analysis, even with sensor plus brain noise. Its intervals were above 1 down to the 25–30~mm bin and below 1 from the 40–45~mm bin, so that Neuromag had the higher detectability for the deeper sources.

Projecting out the room field reduced the ratios of both OPM arrays at depth (Fig.~\ref{fig:depth}A, dashed). The dense array then also had an equal-SNR depth, with bin medians below 1 from the 45–50~mm bin, although its intervals were entirely below 1 only in the 55–60~mm bin. The equal-SNR depth of the site-matched array became shallower, with bin medians below 1 from the 25–30~mm bin and intervals below 1 from the 30–35~mm bin. Whether an OPM array keeps its advantage at depth thus depends on how the room field is treated.

The ratio of the dense array decreased with depth whether or not brain noise was present. The decrease was already present with sensor noise alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:depth}A, dotted) and in the peak field itself, i.e., in the signal without noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:depth}B), whereas adding brain noise multiplied the ratio by a nearly constant factor in every bin (1.51–1.68). Together, these observations suggest that the decrease arose from the source--sensor geometry rather than from the noise.

The dense array had the higher detectability almost everywhere on the cortex, most of all on the lateral convexity (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}). The regional results, however, are exploratory point estimates under the modeled noise. Among the lobes, the advantage of the dense array was largest in the frontal lobe (1.22 [1.18, 1.26]; Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}A). The smallest advantages were found in the cingulate (1.05 [1.04, 1.07]) and the insula (1.09; Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}A). Of the regions chosen from the epilepsy literature, the precentral gyrus favored the dense array most (1.21) and the parahippocampal cortex least (1.12; Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}A), although another 12 atlas parcels, mostly cingulate and medial, had a smaller advantage than the parahippocampal cortex.

The site-matched array had its lowest ratios on ventral and polar surfaces (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}, bottom). Its ratio was 0.71 at the frontal pole and 0.83 at the temporal pole. In the medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortex, it was 0.78 and 0.79, respectively. Among the regions from the literature, the ratio of the site-matched array was lowest in the mesial temporal cortex, the parahippocampal and entorhinal parcels (0.92).

However, the regional ranking of the dense array changed when Neuromag was given its measured noise (scenario D). Under this scenario, the ratio of the dense array became 1.32 in the temporal lobe, the highest of any lobe, and 1.01 in the frontal lobe. The ratio fell below 1 in the cingulate (0.75) and the parietal lobe (0.96). The depth-bin medians of the dense array were also below 1 from the 25–30~mm bin. Overall, the regional ranking depended on the noise model as well as on the array.

\subsection{Smaller heads in the fixed adult helmet}\label{sec:res-heads}

Every principal smaller head showed a larger OPM advantage than the adult when placed in the fixed adult helmet (Fig.~\ref{fig:heads}; Table~\ref{tab:heads}). At top contact, $D$ was +1.50 to +2.13~dB in these heads, compared with +1.00 [+0.82, +1.17]~dB in the adult (Fig.~\ref{fig:heads}A), and the paired change from the adult, $\Delta$, was +0.44 to +1.07~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:heads}B). This excess persisted when the variance of the cortical background per unit area was halved or doubled: the $D$ of every principal head still exceeded that of the adult, by +0.53 to +1.18 and +0.48 to +1.07~dB, respectively (differences of medians, without intervals). The median magnetometer-to-scalp gaps of these heads were also larger, 34.1–40.4~mm, compared with 28.4~mm in the adult (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}A). The larger OPM advantage of these heads was thus accompanied by a larger gap, which the next section separates from head size.

The placement of the head in the helmet, an uncertainty that the parcel-bootstrap intervals do not include, mattered as much as head size. Over the source-blind placements of the fixed helmet, the change from the adult in the school-age scaled adult spanned +0.20 to +1.12~dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}B). This range, computed from differences of medians, is wider than the parcel interval of the change at top contact, [+0.34, +0.56]~dB. \subsection{Helmet fit versus head size}\label{sec:res-fit}

Relative to the fitted helmet, the fixed adult helmet was less favorable to Neuromag in every principal smaller head than in the adult (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}C; Table~\ref{tab:heads}). This interaction was +0.40 to +1.10~dB in the principal heads, with every interval above zero; the two scaled adults defined the extremes of this range, and the templates were intermediate, at +0.54 to +0.66~dB. In the four heads reported separately in Section~S10, the interaction was +0.04 to +0.71~dB, and only the interval of child C included zero (+0.04 [−0.11, +0.19]~dB). Part of the interaction comes from the adult, whose own contrast between the two helmets was negative (−0.23~dB). This negative contrast, consistent with the adult's larger median gap in the fitted helmet (29.6~mm) than at top contact (28.4~mm), is the main reason why the interaction exceeded the within-head contrast of each principal head (+0.17 to +0.76~dB). About half of the adult's contrast, however, arises from the construction of the fitted helmet itself: between top contact and a helmet fitted at the adult's top-contact gap, which have the same median gap, it was −0.11 [−0.15, −0.08]~dB (Section~S5).

When the helmet was fitted to each head and each head had its own dense array, most of the extra OPM advantage of the smaller heads disappeared (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}B; Table~\ref{tab:heads}). With both the smaller head and the adult in helmets fitted at the adult's gap, $\Delta$ was +0.02 to +0.05~dB, with interval bounds of −0.09 to +0.24~dB (Table~\ref{tab:heads}). Every interval included zero except that of the school-age scaled adult, whose lower bound was just above zero. When the helmet was instead fitted at the adult's top-contact gap, these heads retained a change of +0.10 to +0.23~dB relative to the adult at top contact (Section~S5). When the room field was projected out, the change in the fitted helmet was negative in every principal head (−0.25 to −0.05~dB), and only one of the four intervals included zero (Table~S6). In the fitted helmet, the change from the adult therefore amounted to a few hundredths of a decibel, and it became negative once the room field was projected out.

Because the fitted helmet changes the gap, the placement and the coverage of the sensors together, none of these quantities apportions the effects exactly. At equal OPM site count, the change in the fitted helmet increased to +0.20 to +0.49~dB (Table~\ref{tab:heads}). The corresponding changes were +0.02 to +0.05~dB with the array of each head and +0.44 to +1.07~dB in the fixed helmet. Thus, the fitted helmet removed almost all of the extra advantage only because each smaller head had its own, smaller dense array. The comparison suggests that, in the fitted helmet, the scaled adults and templates retained a gain of a few tenths of a decibel from their smaller size, a gain that their fewer OPM sites offset.

\subsection{Simulated interictal spikes}\label{sec:res-spikes}

Under the modeled noise, with idealized detectors and at a nominal false-event rate, the dense array detected simulated superficial spikes at a lower strength than Neuromag in the adult and in every principal smaller head (Fig.~\ref{fig:confirm}A; Table~\ref{tab:spikes}). The declared endpoint, the S50 ratio Neuromag/OPM of the pre-specified run (S50 being the dipole strength at which 50\,\% of the events are detected), was 1.28–1.47 in these anatomies, and every location-bootstrap interval was above 1 (Table~\ref{tab:spikes}). Spikes 10–20~mm deep were thus detected at 22–32\,\% lower strength with the dense array. In the smaller heads, this advantage includes an effect of helmet fit: they were placed at top contact in the fixed adult helmet, whose fit raises their modeled OPM advantage (Section~\ref{sec:res-fit}), and spikes were not simulated in the fitted helmet. As expected when detectability is higher, the declared sign-flip test favored the dense array in each of these anatomies (p < 0.0001 in each of these five, Holm-adjusted over the nine declared anatomies; Table~S11). In the adult, the S50 ratio (1.45) was of the order of the detectability ratio at these depths (1.36--1.60; Fig.~\ref{fig:depth}A), so the detectability advantage carried over to the detection of these spikes.

The S50 ratio changed little across noise replicates, with the oracle and mismatched detectors, and with re-fitted thresholds (Fig.~\ref{fig:confirm}B, C; Table~\ref{tab:spikes}). Over the five noise replicates, its standard deviation was 2.9–5.4\,\%, and the pooled ratios were 1.25–1.44 (Table~\ref{tab:spikes}). The oracle detector, whose threshold does not depend on the calibration, favored the dense array by a similar or larger factor (1.39–1.62; Fig.~\ref{fig:confirm}B), at a realized false-positive probability per trial of 0.00093–0.0011 on held-out null data. Thresholds re-fitted on held-out null data to the nominal 1 false event per minute changed the ratios little (1.27–1.50). The mismatched detector, which departs from the simulation in waveform, forward model and coregistration, gave S50 ratios of 1.39–1.50 (Fig.~\ref{fig:confirm}C). Relative to the endpoint, these ratios changed by a factor of 0.97–1.11, every interval of this change included 1 (Table~S14), and the test favored the dense array in all five anatomies, with frozen and with re-fitted thresholds.

On separate null data, the frozen thresholds produced 1.00–1.40 false events per minute for the dense array and 1.05–2.25 for Neuromag. An approximate equal-rate test, which we left uncorrected because the arrays share their background and room noise, had p < 0.05 in one of these anatomies, the 24-month template. In that anatomy, Neuromag had the higher false-event rate. A departure in this direction would, if anything, understate the advantage of the dense array in the declared endpoint. After re-fitting, the rates were 1.00–1.60 for the dense array and 0.70–1.45 for Neuromag. The test then had p < 0.05 in one anatomy, the school-age scaled adult, in which the dense array had the higher rate, so that its re-fitted ratio would, if anything, overstate the advantage of the dense array in this secondary analysis (Section~S7).

The site-matched array had smaller S50 ratios than the dense array (1.06–1.30; Table~\ref{tab:spikes}), consistent with its detectability advantage being limited to shallow sources (Section~\ref{sec:res-depth}). The exploratory run agreed with the pre-specified run: in the same depth band, its S50 ratios for the dense array were 1.36–1.65 over 18 locations per anatomy (Fig.~\ref{fig:confirm}A, light markers). The deeper bands of that run are reported in Section~S7. In the pre-specified run, the four heads reported separately in Section~S10 had ratios of 1.27–1.43. Under the modeled noise and at a nominal false-event rate, the dense array therefore detected superficial spikes at a lower strength than Neuromag with every detector, with frozen and re-fitted thresholds, and in both runs.

In contrast to detection, no difference in localization between the arrays survived a correction over all comparisons in the exploratory run. That run covered all nine anatomies, including the four reported separately in Section~S10. Median errors of 3.7–7.6~mm were found for dipole fits of detected 320-nAm focal spikes in every array and anatomy, of the order of the coregistration displacement (2.1–3.2~mm). Of 360 paired comparisons of dipole and dSPM errors and of joint detection and localization, 65 reached p < 0.05 uncorrected. By chance, about 18 would be expected. Most of these nominally significant comparisons favored an OPM array (61). However, only eight survived a Bonferroni correction within anatomy and array, and none survived one over all 360 (Section~S7; Fig.~S14).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

In our simulations of a realistic adult head, the comparison between on-scalp OPMs and the Neuromag system depended on the array, the OPM noise floor, the Neuromag channels used as comparator and the SNR measure. Brain noise turned the advantage of the SQUIDs into an advantage of a dense OPM array. That advantage, however, was as large as the model's error for Neuromag's noise. When the helmet was fitted to the adult's gap and each head had its own dense array, most of the extra OPM advantage of smaller heads disappeared. Under the modeled noise, the detectability difference carried over to the detection of superficial simulated spikes. Together, these results suggest that proximity alone does not set the on-scalp advantage: how the arrays are built, how quiet the sensors are and how well the SQUID helmet fits determine whether it appears and how large it is.

\subsection{What determines the adult comparison}\label{sec:disc-noise}

The spherical analysis of \citet{jas2026}, which we reimplemented in Section~S2 (Fig.~S1), places the OPM advantage within an equal-SNR depth set by an assumed noise ratio. Under the modeled sensor and brain noise, however, the dense array had an advantage at every depth. When the room field was projected out, the dense array showed an equal-SNR depth (from the 45–50~mm bin), as it did, in point estimates, when Neuromag was given its measured noise (from the 25–30~mm bin, close to the 28~mm of the sphere). Whether the dense advantage extends to deep sources therefore depends on the noise model, whereas the site-matched array had an equal-SNR depth even with sensor plus brain noise. The OPM noise floor and the array design, in contrast, can reverse which system has the higher detectability over the whole cortex.

The reversal by brain noise is consistent with each channel's noise budget. Brain noise raises the noise of a Neuromag magnetometer far more than that of an OPM, relative to their sensor floors (Section~\ref{sec:res-adult}). The OPM's higher sensor noise therefore matters less once brain noise dominates. The comparator and the SNR measure then determine which system a reported SNR favors: a peak-channel SNR against magnetometers favors the OPM, one against the best of all channels favors Neuromag, and a channel-averaged SNR shows little difference.

The size of the dense advantage cannot be determined more precisely than the model's error for Neuromag's noise allows. The five noise scenarios are assumptions about how that error is shared, not bounds on it. Which of them comes closest cannot be decided here. Scenario D, which gives Neuromag its measured noise and the OPM its modeled noise, is not the case least favorable to the OPM, because OPMs are magnetometers and their real noise may share some of the far-field excess that the model misses for Neuromag's magnetometers. The break-even levels inherit this dependence, because they were computed for single-axis arrays with white OPM noise and modeled Neuromag noise. If Neuromag's measured noise applies, the dense array has no clear advantage at the primary OPM noise. Its break-even level would then lie above that noise by an amount that the medians cannot determine, whereas that of the site-matched array would lie below it, if within the sweep at all (Section~\ref{sec:res-noise}). To place a real array relative to Neuromag, one would therefore need to enter its measured noise covariance into the full model, with matched geometry, sensing axes and preprocessing. A colored and correlated covariance cannot be reduced to the single white-noise level of the sweep.

\subsection{Head size and helmet fit}\label{sec:disc-fit}

The spherical analysis predicts a larger OPM benefit in smaller heads, because SQUID sensors at a constant distance from the scalp leave more of a small brain within the equal-SNR depth \citep{jas2026}. In the fixed adult helmet, the scaled adults and the two templates did show a larger OPM advantage than the adult. When the helmet was fitted to each head at the adult's gap and each head had its own dense array, however, only a few hundredths of a decibel of this excess remained. In every principal head, the fixed helmet was less favorable to Neuromag, relative to the fitted helmet, than in the adult (Section~\ref{sec:res-fit}). Because this comparison involves two Neuromag helmets around the same OPM array, it does not depend on the unmeasured OPM noise, although it does depend on the modeled Neuromag noise and on the anatomy. The comparison of the two helmets thus suggests that, in this model, the prediction of the sphere holds in the adult helmet but largely reflects how that helmet fits a smaller head, through its gap, placement and coverage together. The construction does not, however, isolate the median gap, or exclude head size, as a cause.

The scaled adults are the main support for our conclusion, because they preserve the adult anatomy (Table~\ref{tab:heads}). What remains in the fitted helmet depends on the adult's reference placement (Section~\ref{sec:res-fit}) and, for the templates, on their scalp convention (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). This small residual, however, masks a gain from head size itself. When the OPM site count was equalized, the change in the fitted helmet was a few tenths of a decibel, up to about half of the fixed-helmet excess in the templates. With brain noise included, this change is the analog of the head-size gain that \citet{zahran2022} found with sensor noise alone in a helmet at a constant gap. However, the gain is offset by the fewer OPM sites of the smaller heads, and in each principal head it is smaller than the interaction.

We found this gain in uniformly scaled heads and in smooth infant templates, whose cortex shrinks with the head. In contrast, the school-aged children of Section~S10, with similar head circumferences, had nearly adult cortical areas (1,666–1,869~cm$^{2}$). At equal OPM site count, children A and B kept a smaller gain (+0.16 and +0.16~dB, with intervals including zero), which their fewer OPM sites outweighed, so that with their own arrays the change in the fitted helmet was negative (−0.30 and −0.39~dB). Their near-scalp anatomy could not be verified, but they suggest that the gain may be smaller in individual children than in scaled heads and templates.

For pediatric MEG, these results suggest that much of the extra on-scalp advantage that small heads show in an adult helmet reflects the fit of that helmet. Our fitted helmet retains the adult's sensors and does not model a pediatric SQUID system, such as one built for young children \citep{tesan2010}. A child's position in an adult helmet matters as well \citep{gaetz2008}. Three of the four heads reported separately also had a clearly positive interaction, but child C did not, and the three school-aged children gained less in the fixed helmet than the principal heads (Section~S10).

\subsection{Simulated spikes and the clinical literature}\label{sec:disc-clinical}

Under the modeled noise and with idealized detectors, the dense array detected superficial spikes at a lower strength than Neuromag in the adult and in each principal smaller head. In the adult, the only head in which we compared the two, the S50 ratio was of the order of the detectability ratio at those depths. In the adult and in every principal smaller head, the ranking was the same with the mismatched detector, the oracle detector and re-fitted thresholds. Thus, the spike simulations illustrate that the modeled detectability difference carries over to detection at a nominal false-event rate. They do not, however, test the noise model. Nor do they show how detection would fare under the noise alternatives of Section~\ref{sec:res-noise}. In contrast to detection, no single localization comparison survived a correction over all such comparisons, although nominally significant ones were more frequent than expected by chance and almost all favored an OPM array. The dipole-fit errors were of the order of the coregistration error, which may have dominated the comparison.

The clinical reports and our model are compatible, but the model cannot identify which factor operated in any study. Table~S19 sets each study beside its closest model analog. For example, higher or similar OPM SNR against SQUID magnetometers (\citealp{feys2022}; mixed results in \citealp{feys2025}) corresponds to the peak-channel SNR of the model against the magnetometers. In the adult and in every principal smaller head, this SNR favors the OPM, as it does in the heads of Section~S10 (Section~S6). In contrast, equal or lower SNR against planar gradiometers or the nearest channels of few or noisier sensors \citep{hillebrand2023,schwartz2025} corresponds to peak-channel SNR against the gradiometers or the best channel. The larger gains reported in school-aged children recorded in an adult helmet \citep{feys2022} are consistent with the fixed-helmet effect in our scaled adults and templates. The three school-aged children we modeled, however, showed the smallest such effect (Section~S10).

The weaker OPM results for temporal and mesial temporal sources \citep{feys2024,feys2025,shen2026} have two analogs in our model. One is the weakness of deep sources for every system. The other, the deficit of the site-matched array on ventral and polar surfaces, is fragile, because the model overstates Neuromag's detectability in the temporal lobe (Section~\ref{sec:res-noise}). More generally, the regional ranking is exploratory and changes with the noise model. When Neuromag was given its measured noise (scenario D), the temporal lobe had the highest ratio of the dense array, and the cingulate and parietal ratios fell below 1 (Section~\ref{sec:res-depth}), so no regional recommendation follows from this ranking.

We did not model clinical dual- or triaxial arrays, so the model does not determine where they would fall. The single-axis array at the Neuromag sites had no whole-cortex advantage at the primary noise level and a disadvantage at higher OPM noise. Small advantages of this array appeared only at lower OPM noise, with some comparators and SNR measures, with a plug-in covariance, or within about 30~mm of the scalp. When three equally quiet axes were used at the same sites (294 channels), its ratio came close to that of the dense array (Section~\ref{sec:res-adult}). The triaxial result suggests that the number of independent measurements, gained from more sites or from more axes per site, mattered more than the site count alone, provided the extra axes are as quiet as the normal one. The modest whole-cortex advantage of the dense array was obtained with more sites than any array used in the epilepsy studies of Table~S19.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations remain, most of which point to a specific measurement or simulation. The most important concerns the noise model: no measurement validates the OPM covariance, noise specific to OPMs (cross-talk, gain and sensitive-axis errors) was not modeled, and the model was calibrated and checked on one recording whose magnetometer noise it partly misses. Because the advantage of the dense array is as large as the model's error for Neuromag, these limitations could reverse the adult comparison, whereas the omitted OPM noise does not enter the interaction. A measured OPM covariance and recordings from further subjects would test the advantage and the generality of the error. In addition, one projection stands in for the preprocessing of real systems \citep{taulu2006,tierney2021}, and clinical dual- and triaxial arrays were not modeled. The pediatric conclusion rests on one adult, two uniformly scaled copies of it and two templates (Section~\ref{sec:disc-fit}), and a cohort of individual pediatric MRIs would test it. The scalp convention of the templates also differs from the adult's by 2.6–3.0~mm (Section~\ref{sec:heads}), which could bias their $D$, $\Delta$ and fitted-helmet residual (+0.04 and +0.05~dB) by an unquantified amount; its effect on the interaction, expected to be smaller, was not tested. The smaller heads keep the adult's background variance per unit area (Section~\ref{sec:res-heads}), and all sources lie on the cortical surface. In every head, the primary model omits the cortex within 4~mm of the inner skull, which includes the gyral crowns nearest the sensors; only the adult's sensitivity analyses added it back from 2~mm. Finally, the spike detectors use the stationary model of the simulation, whose null data hold no physiological transients, and the threshold-calibration uncertainty is not propagated into the S50 intervals. Spikes were simulated neither under the noise alternatives nor in the fitted helmet, which recordings of real or phantom spikes with both systems would address.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

This simulation study compared on-scalp OPM arrays with a cryogenic Neuromag system under explicit sensor, brain and room noise. In the adult, the array design and the OPM noise floor determined which system had the higher detectability. The comparator and the SNR measure determined which system a reported SNR favored. The modeled advantage of a dense single-axis array was as large as the model's error for Neuromag's noise. Its break-even OPM noise of 31.5~fT/√Hz therefore holds only for the modeled Neuromag noise; with Neuromag's measured noise, the dense array had no clear advantage at 15~fT/√Hz. A single-axis array at the Neuromag sites showed no clear difference from Neuromag, whereas three equally quiet axes at those sites came close to the dense array; clinical dual- and triaxial arrays, however, were not modeled. When each smaller head kept its own dense array, fitting the SQUID helmet removed most of the extra OPM advantage that the adult helmet produced. Under the modeled noise, idealized detectors detected weaker superficial spikes with the dense array than with Neuromag at a nominal false-event rate. Measured OPM covariances would test the adult comparison, and comparisons in children depend on how well the SQUID helmet fits.
