**Aim (paraphrased; pp. 2, 5).** For event-related fields, on-scalp OPMs and off-scalp SQUIDs differ much less in SNR than in raw signal amplitude. The paper sets out to explain why, and looks at two factors:

1. **How the signal depends on source depth.** This part is simulated with a tangential current dipole in a spherical head. The simulations vary the OPM/SQUID noise ratio η and the head size, from newborn to adult. The key output is the *equal-SNR source depth* d_eq: at depths greater than d_eq, off-scalp sensors have the higher SNR.
2. **How the noise depends on scalp-to-sensor distance.** This part is measured. The authors recorded median-nerve SEFs (N20) with OPM and SQUID arrays placed at several scalp-to-sensor distances. The measurement is complemented by a three-dipole toy simulation (Fig. 6): one target dipole, compared with either a deeper or a more superficial "noise" dipole.

The stated goal is to clarify the complementary SNR properties of OPMs and SQUIDs for specific subject populations and brain regions (p. 5).

### 2.2 Equations (transcribed)

**Radial field of a current dipole.** This equation is unnumbered (p. 8), and the PDF prints it without the parentheses around **Q** × **r**_Q.

```
B_r(r_Q, r_s) = (μ0/4π) · ((Q × r_Q) · e_r) / |r_s − r_Q|^3
```

**Sensor position.** Unnumbered (p. 8).

```
r_s = s e_r = s (sin θ, 0, cos θ)^T ,   θ ∈ [0, π]
```

**Angle of the topography peak.** Obtained from ∂B_r/∂θ = 0; unnumbered (p. 8).

```
cos θ0 = −γ + sqrt(γ² + 3) ,   γ = (s² + r_Q²) / (2 s r_Q)
```

**Eq. (1): peak radial field.** The paper cites Boto et al. 2017 for this expression and uses it as the "signal" (pp. 8–9).

```
B_r^max(r_Q, s) = (μ0/4π) · Q r_Q sqrt(1 − cos²θ0) / [s² + r_Q² − 2 s r_Q cos θ0]^(3/2)
```

**Eqs. (2a) and (2b): SNR** (p. 9). The paper calls σ² the "noise power". σ itself is the noise amplitude, so the SNR is an amplitude ratio.

```
SNR_OPM   = B_r^max(r_Q, h)     / σ_OPM        (2a)
SNR_SQUID = B_r^max(r_Q, h + ξ) / σ_SQUID      (2b)
```

**Relative noise level.** Unnumbered (p. 9). The paper assumes **η ≥ 1**, meaning OPM noise is never lower than SQUID noise; the reasons are given in the Discussion (p. 22).

```
η = σ_OPM / σ_SQUID
```

**Eq. (3): equal-SNR depth** (p. 9). d_eq is the depth at which SNR_OPM = SNR_SQUID for a given η. It is defined only if it lies inside the brain, **h − b ≤ d_eq < h**.

```
B_r^max(h − d_eq, h) / B_r^max(h − d_eq, h + ξ) = η          (3)
```

**Experimental SNR.** Unnumbered (p. 12).

```
SNR_N20 = B_N20 / σ_N20
```

### 2.3 Parameter sweeps (p. 9)

- Dipole depth d from deep to superficial.
- **η from 1 to 6.**
- Head size from infant to adult (Table 1).
- To relate the model to the SEF experiment, ξ from on-scalp **to 6 cm** off-scalp. Section 8, item 14 shows that Fig. 6 actually plots only 0–50 mm.

The grids are **not stated**, for both depth and η. Figs. 3–5 are consistent with r_Q = linspace(0, b, 101)[1:], i.e. steps of b/100 (0.8 mm for the adult), which excludes r_Q = 0 **[recovered]**.

### 2.4 Silent or excluded sources and the depth domain

- **Radial dipoles.** Only a tangential dipole is modeled. The paper never discusses radial dipoles, which are silent in a sphere (**not stated**).
- **Origin.** A dipole at the sphere origin (r_Q = 0, d = h) gives zero signal in every sensor. The paper says this happens at d = 95 mm for the adult (p. 14). Eq. 1 cannot be evaluated directly at r_Q = 0: γ → ∞, so cos θ0 = −γ + √(γ² + 3) takes the form ∞ − ∞. The limit of B_r^max is 0 **[derived]**. The published curves stop at r_Q = b/100, which is d = 94.2 mm for the adult **[recovered]**.
- **Sources outside the brain sphere.** Sources in the CSF/skull/scalp layer (d < h − b) are excluded. The model considers only 0 ≤ r_Q ≤ b (p. 6), and d_eq is defined only for h − b ≤ d_eq < h (p. 9). The layer thickness h − b is Table 1's "combined thickness of CSF, skull, and scalp" (p. 7).
- **Absolute noise.** The absolute noise level is **not stated**; only η enters the model. `replicate_figure3.py` recovered σ_SQUID = B_r^max(0.8b, h + 18 mm) = **0.3546 pT**, which gives σ_OPM = 3 × 0.3546 = 1.064 pT at η = 3. This σ_SQUID equals the Fig. 6 superficial "noise dipole" B3 evaluated at ξ = 18 mm **[recovered]**. Figs. 4A–D keep σ_SQUID fixed, so the SQUID curve is identical in every panel, and set σ_OPM = η·σ_SQUID **[fig]**.

### 2.5 Derived properties useful when re-implementing [derived]

- **Small-r_Q limit.** As r_Q → 0, θ0 → 90° and B_r^max → (μ0/4π)·Q·r_Q/s³. The OPM/SQUID ratio in Eq. 3 therefore tends to **η0 = ((h + ξ)/h)³**.
- **Uniqueness of d_eq.** The ratio R(r_Q) = B_r^max(r_Q, h)/B_r^max(r_Q, h + ξ) is strictly increasing in r_Q; I checked this numerically for all four head models. So d_eq is unique, and it exists if and only if **η0 < η ≤ η1**, where η1 = R(b).
- **Adult thresholds.** η0 = (113/95)³ = **1.6829** and η1 = **5.3086**. The paper prints 1.7 and 5.3 (p. 15).
- **Exact adult d_eq (h = 95, b = 80, ξ = 18):**

## 4. The target-versus-background ("toy") depth experiment: Fig. 6 (pp. 16–17; discussed on p. 25)

### 4.1 Setup

- **Purpose.** The simulation fixes the source and varies ξ. Both the signal and the noise depend on ξ, which mimics a situation where brain noise dominates (p. 16).
- **Head model.** Adult sphere, h = 95 mm (Fig. 6 caption, p. 17). By Table 1, b = 80 mm.
- **Quantities.** B1, B2 and B3 are each the peak radial field B_r^max from Eq. 1, evaluated on a sensor sphere of radius s = h + ξ.
- **Depths as printed.** The text (p. 16) gives d1 = 32 mm, d2 = 48 mm and "d3 = 64", with no unit. The caption and legend (p. 17) give **d1 = 63 mm, d2 = 47 mm and d3 = 31 mm**. The caption values are the correct ones: they reproduce the plotted curves (§4.3). The text values are the r_Q values, r_Q = 32, 48 and 64 mm, which equal **0.4b, 0.6b and 0.8b** **[derived]**.
- **Roles (p. 17):**
  - **Target:** B2, at d2 = 47 mm and r_Q = 0.6b.
  - **Deep noise source:** B1, at d1 = 63 mm and r_Q = 0.4b.
  - **Superficial noise source:** B3, at d3 = 31 mm and **r_Q = 0.8b = 64 mm**.
- **Dipole moments and orientation.**
  - The moments of the noise dipoles are **not stated**. The plotted values are reproduced when all three dipoles are 30 nAm **[derived]**.
  - The inset shows all three dipoles pointing along +y on the z-axis.
- **Range of ξ.** The Methods (p. 9) say ξ runs from 0 to 6 cm, but the curves in the figure span **0 to ≈50 mm** **[fig]**.
- **Link to Figs. 3–4.** At ξ = 18 mm, B3 = **0.3546 pT**. This is the σ_SQUID recovered for Figs. 3–4 **[recovered]**.

### 4.2 What "SNR" means here

- **Definition.** SNR(ξ) = B2(ξ)/B1(ξ) (deep noise) or B2(ξ)/B3(ξ) (superficial noise). The y-label is "SNR" and the legend reads "B2 / B1" and "B2 / B3".
- **What is compared.** Each B is the peak of that dipole's **own** radial topography on the same sphere. The ratio therefore compares a target peak with the peak of a single interfering dipole; it is not a ratio at one sensor and not a variance ratio. If all moments are equal, the ratio does not depend on Q.
- **Authors' framing.** The Discussion (p. 25) restates the assumption: the noise level varies with ξ in the same way as the field of a current dipole at a fixed depth. It also concedes that a single dipole is not a realistic model of brain noise (pp. 17, 25).

### 4.3 Results

**Printed results (p. 17).**

- All signals increase as the sensors approach the scalp, which is the right-hand side of the reversed x-axis.
- **B2/B1** (noise deeper than the target; blue curve) is **highest on-scalp** (ξ = 0).
- **B2/B3** (noise more superficial; red dashed curve) is **higher off-scalp** (ξ > 0) than on-scalp.
- The authors' explanation: as ξ changes, the relative change in source-to-sensor distance is larger for the superficial noise source than for the target.

### 5.5 Processing and analysis (p. 12)

- **Software.** OPM and SQUID data were processed identically in MNE-Python (Gramfort et al. 2013).
- **Pre-processing.**
  - **High-pass filter at 4 Hz.**
  - **Homogeneous field compensation (HFC)** (Tierney et al. 2021), applied to both systems.
  - Stimulus artefacts and large transients were excluded with the MNE annotation tool.
- **Averaging.**
  - Epochs run from **−100 to +50 ms** relative to the stimulus.
  - The baseline is **−100 to −5 ms**.
  - For each sensor type, the N20 latency was taken from the closest-distance runs; **B_N20** is the amplitude at that latency in every run.
  - The **N20 latency is 24 ms**; this appears only in the Fig. 7B map labels (p. 18).
- **Noise.** **σ_N20 is defined as the standard deviation, across trials, of the unaveraged data at the N20 latency** (p. 12). The Fig. 8 caption instead calls it the "standard error of mean" (p. 19). See §8, item 16.
- **SNR.** SNR_N20 = B_N20/σ_N20, computed for each run and sensor.
- **PSD.** The PSD of the continuous data was computed. The method, window and dB reference are **not stated**. The Fig. 9 axis reads "fT²/Hz (dB)".
- **Not stated:**
  - the number of trials remaining after rejection;
  - the HFC order or settings;
  - any line-noise filtering. Fig. 9 shows a narrow dip at 60 Hz, which suggests a notch filter **[fig]**;
  - any low-pass filter applied during analysis.

## 6. Gradiometers, brain noise and the limits of the point-magnetometer model (Discussion, pp. 21–25)

- **Why off-scalp sensors can win for deep sources (pp. 21–22).** The authors name two contributions.
  1. The signal ratio between superficial and deep sources is larger for on-scalp sensors, because the signal falls faster with depth. Fig. 4D shows this, since the SNR and signal curves differ only by a constant factor.
  2. The noise is higher on-scalp.
     - Intrinsic noise is higher in OPMs and high-Tc SQUIDs than in liquid-helium SQUIDs (Brookes et al. 2022).
     - In conventional SQUIDs, brain noise normally limits sensitivity (Hämäläinen et al. 1993).
     - Brain noise is larger for on-scalp sensors because they are closer to its sources.
     - Environmental noise probably affects both sensor types about equally. The exception is implants that move relative to SQUIDs but not relative to OPMs (Safar et al. 2024).
- **SNR-based versus lead-field-based sensitivity (p. 22).**
  - Detectability should account for realistic brain noise (Goldenholz et al. 2009; Hunold et al. 2016), not only lead-field sensitivity (Hillebrand & Barnes 2002).
  - Contrast in the cerebellum: in lead-field terms, OPMs measure stronger signals across the cerebellum (Roos, Hämäläinen & Iivanainen 2026). SNR-based analysis finds a benefit mainly for its superficial parts (Matsubara et al. 2026).
- **Pediatric relevance (pp. 22–23).**
  - Fixed adult-size SQUID helmets cause large distances and head movement in children.
  - Fig. 5 suggests the SNR benefit of on-scalp sensors is largest for small heads.
  - The model still uses the same 18-mm SQUID stand-off for every head.
- **Stated limitations of the model (pp. 23–24).**
  - A single current dipole.
  - A spherically symmetric head.
  - Magnetometers that detect only the radial component.
  - Brain noise not modeled beyond the assumption that it decreases with distance.
  - The authors expect these choices to shift d_eq but not to change the main conclusions.
- **Why the dipole and sphere are defended (p. 24).**
  - Dipoles represent focal activity, and extended or distributed sources are sums of dipoles (Ahlfors et al. 2010; Hämäläinen & Ilmoniemi 1994).
  - A sphere fitted to the local skull curvature is useful (Huang et al. 1999).
  - In realistic geometries the idea generalizes to an **"equal-SNR surface"** that splits source space by which sensor type has the higher SNR (Vorwerk et al. 2014). Matsubara et al. 2026 show this for the cerebellum.
- **Field components (p. 24).**
  - Most SQUID systems measure roughly the normal component, which corresponds to B_r.
  - Many OPMs measure several components (Brookes et al. 2021).
  - Tangential components have different topographies but also fall off nonlinearly with distance, so the SNR behavior should be qualitatively similar.
- **Planar gradiometers (pp. 24–25).**
  - The authors draw a partial analogy: on-scalp versus off-scalp resembles gradiometer versus magnetometer, and an equal-SNR depth can apply to both comparisons.
  - Gradiometer signals fall faster with depth than magnetometer signals, especially for planar gradiometers (Cuffin & Cohen 1979).
  - Qualitatively, Fig. 4's blue "OPM" curves could stand for **planar gradiometers** and the red "SQUID" curves for **magnetometers** at the **same** scalp-to-sensor distance.
  - Hunold et al. (2016) simulated realistic brain noise and epileptic spikes. They found higher SNR for planar gradiometers with superficial sources and no significant difference for deep sources, which the authors liken to Fig. 4A.
  - The paper gives **no quantitative gradiometer model**: no baseline, no coil geometry, no gradiometer noise.
- **Point-magnetometer and finite-size limits (p. 8).** The model ignores the finite SQUID coils and OPM cells over which the field is integrated. The Discussion does not quantify how much this matters.
- **Future work (p. 25).**
  - Larger OPM arrays, more participants, anatomically realistic head models, and better noise reduction and signal processing, including beamformers (Boto et al. 2016).
  - Because on-scalp sensors are more sensitive to superficial sources, more detailed forward models may be needed, for example for skull defects (Lau et al. 2016; Lew et al. 2021; Vorwerk et al. 2014). Conductivity deviations can appear as virtual sources (Ahlfors et al. 2026).
- **Brain noise (p. 25).**
  - The key open question is how brain noise varies with scalp-to-sensor distance.
  - Fig. 6 assumed the noise scales like the field of one dipole at a fixed depth, which is not a realistic spatial distribution.
  - Brain-noise fields vary with frequency band, population, individual and state (Marzetti et al. 2013). They should be characterized (Bijma & de Munck 2008; Engemann & Gramfort 2015; Hunold et al. 2016).

    - Whether the PSDs were computed before or after the 4-Hz high-pass. p. 20 describes them as PSDs of the raw data.

25. **HFC on SQUIDs.** HFC was applied to the SQUID magnetometers too, since p. 12 says both systems were processed identically. This is unusual; decide whether to replicate it.

*D. Model details and typography*

26. **Sampling grids and the d_eq rule.**
    - Neither the depth grid, the η grid nor the rule for extracting d_eq is stated.
    - Figs. 3–5 are consistent with r_Q = linspace(0, b, 101)[1:] and "the deepest grid depth with SNR_OPM > SNR_SQUID".
    - With that rule, η ≤ η0 is plotted at the deepest grid point (h − b/100), and η > η1 is not plotted.
    - Fig. 5's adult curve ends at η ≈ 5.25 **[recovered/fig]**.
27. **The origin.** Eq. 1 cannot be evaluated directly at r_Q = 0 (γ → ∞), although its limit there is 0. The text (p. 14) says the signal vanishes at the origin (d = 95 mm), but the curves stop at d = 94.2 mm **[recovered]**.
28. **Absolute noise level.** Not stated. The SNR axes in Figs. 3B and 4 imply σ_SQUID = B_r^max(0.8b, h + 18 mm) = 0.3546 pT **[recovered]**. This is the SQUID signal of the Fig. 6 superficial dipole.
29. **Noise "power".** σ² is called noise power (p. 9) but the SNR divides by σ, so the SNR is an amplitude ratio.
30. **SQUID stand-off for all heads.** The model uses 18 mm for every head size (p. 16), although the Discussion (pp. 22–23) points out that adult-size helmets leave children with larger distances. A pediatric extension must choose between keeping 18 mm and using larger stand-offs.
31. **18 mm versus "about 20 mm".** The simulations use 18 mm (p. 8), which is the Dewar wall thickness (p. 11); the Introduction (p. 3) says conventional SQUIDs reach a minimum of about 20 mm. The 18 mm is a lower bound, not a typical distance.
32. **Typography.**
    - The unnumbered B_r formula lacks parentheses around **Q** × **r**_Q (p. 8).
    - A stray word "theta" follows θ ∈ [0, π] (p. 8).
    - Subscripts "N20" are truncated to "N2" (p. 19 text and Fig. 8 caption).
    - "stimulations" appears for simulations (p. 23), and "stimulation findings" for simulation findings (p. 21).
    - "scalp-to-sensor sensor distances" (p. 15).
    - "effects of scalp-to-sensor" is missing the word "distance" (p. 12).
